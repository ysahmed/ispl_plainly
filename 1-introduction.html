Introduction

The opening chapter makes no models and fits nothing. It introduces the field, walks through the three datasets the rest of the book keeps coming back to, gives a five-minute history, and — the part that earns its page — decodes the notation every later chapter assumes you already know.

Reading

  • Read Chapter 1 in full — it is short, and none of it is hard. Skip nothing except the matrix display in §5 if it makes your eyes glaze; the sentence underneath it is enough.
  • Datasets Wage · Smarket · NCI60 (all ship with the ISLP package)

Key ideas

Statistical learning means estimating a relationship from data instead of knowing it. Three questions run through the whole book: is there an answer to predict (supervised), is that answer a number or a category (regression or classification), and if there is no answer at all, what structure is left to find (unsupervised). Chapter 1 also fixes five symbols — n, p, xij, xi, yi — that mean exactly the same thing in every chapter that follows.

1. What statistical learning is

Statistical learning is a set of tools for working out a relationship from data, when nobody has told you what that relationship is. You observe inputs and you observe outcomes, and the job is to find the rule hiding between them — well enough that it still works on data you have not seen.

Everything in the book is sorted by two questions, and it is worth learning the four boxes now, because every method later gets filed into one of them:

The answer is a numberThe answer is a category
We have answers
supervised learning		 Regression — how much? Predict a person's wage from their age. Classification — which one? Predict whether the market goes up or down.
No answers
unsupervised learning		 Clustering — who is like whom? Find groups among 64 cell lines when nobody has labelled them. There is no “right answer” to be scored against, only structure to discover.

There is also a fifth box the book mentions in passing: semi-supervised learning, where a handful of rows are labelled and the rest are not — common in medicine and biology, where labels are expensive. We will not need it again until it comes up naturally.

The one sentence to remember

We never get to see the true relationship — only its output, with noise mixed in. Every method in this book is a different guess about what is behind the numbers, and the rest of the book is about choosing between those guesses.

2. Three datasets, three shapes of problem

The chapter introduces three real datasets, and it does so carefully: each one exists to make one of those four boxes concrete. They reappear throughout the book, so it pays to know what each one looks like now.

  • Wage — 3,000 men from the mid-Atlantic US, 11 variables each (year, age, race, education, job class, health…). We have an answer: their wage. Predicting a number, so this is regression.
  • Smarket — 1,250 days of the S&P 500 with the previous five days' percentage changes. The answer is Up or Down. Predicting a category, so this is classification.
  • NCI60 — 64 cancer cell lines, each measured on 6,830 genes. There is no answer to predict; we only want to know which cell lines resemble each other. This is unsupervised.

Two details in that chart are worth pausing on, because the chapter makes them deliberately:

  • The wage curve bends. It rises with age, then flattens and falls after about 60. A straight line would miss the bend entirely — the failure is built into the shape of the guess, not into the data.
  • The stock boxes overlap. If two groups look the same, no method can separate them well, and pretending otherwise is how people lose money. Chapter 4 will squeeze about 60% accuracy out of this data — barely better than a coin, and the book says so out loud.

n and p, already

You have met the book's basic units without being told: Wage is n = 3,000 rows and p = 11 columns; NCI60 is n = 64 and p = 6,830. Rows are people (or days, or cell lines); columns are the measurements taken on them. Section 5 gives these their symbols for good.

3. A brief history

The phrase statistical learning is recent, but almost everything in it is old. The field is best understood as the moment when methods that already existed in statistics became cheap enough to run on modern computers — and therefore available to everyone.

WhenWhat appearedWhere it lives in this book
early 1800sLeast squares — the original linear regression, invented for tracking planets and stars Chapter 3
1936Linear discriminant analysis, for predicting categories instead of numbersChapter 4
1940sLogistic regression — the other half of classificationChapter 4
early 1970sGeneralized linear models: one framework containing both of the aboveChapter 4 §6
1980sTrees, then generalized additive models, then neural networks — non-linear methods finally became computable Chapters 7, 8, 10
1990sSupport vector machinesChapter 9
nowFree, friendly software (Python) turns the whole toolkit into something anyone can runevery lab

Notice the shape of that list: for a century there were only linear methods, because non-linear ones could not be computed. When computing power arrived, the field did not replace the old methods — it grew around them. Linear methods are still the default recommendation for most real work, and Chapters 3 and 4 are the ones you will use most.

4. Who this book is for

ISLP is the accessible sibling of The Elements of Statistical Learning (ESL), by the same first three authors. ESL is comprehensive and technical; ISLP deliberately is not. The authors state their premises plainly, and they are worth repeating because they explain how this site is written too:

1 Useful far beyond statistics. The book covers the methods that turn out to matter in business, biology, engineering and the social sciences — not every method that exists.
2 No black boxes. You should know what each method is doing and what it assumes, or you cannot choose between them. Understanding the cogs is required; building the machine is not.
3 The maths is deliberately thin. Matrix algebra is almost entirely avoided. You are not expected to derive anything, and one introductory statistics course is enough background.
4 Meant to be run, not just read. Every chapter ends with a Python lab on real data. Roughly a third of the course time in the authors' own classes goes on the labs.

The audience the authors name is broad: scientists, engineers, analysts, data scientists, quants, plus people from business and the social sciences who are comfortable with numbers but not necessarily with mathematics. In other words — this book was written for you, notation and all.

About the flagged passages

The book marks a handful of harder passages with a small icon. They are optional. Skipping them loses nothing that later chapters depend on, and this site marks them too whenever they come up.

5. Notation: the five symbols you need

Everything the book does later is written in a handful of symbols. They look like algebra, but each one is just a label for an everyday idea — a row, a column, a single cell. Here they are, one at a time, against a real table of five people from the Wage data.

Three things the symbols stand for:

$$n$$ How many rows — people, days, cell lines. The size of the sample. Wage has n = 3,000.
$$p$$ How many columns — variables we measured. The number of things we know about each row. Wage has p = 11.
$$x_{ij}$$ One cell: the value of variable j for observation i. “Age of person 3” — row first, column second.
$$x_i$$ A whole row — everything measured on one person. “Person 3's file.”
$$y_i$$ The answer for row i, and only that: one number per row. “Person 3's wage.” This is the thing we are trying to predict.

The whole table of n rows and p columns is written as a big grid called X:

$$X = \begin{pmatrix} x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{pmatrix}$$

Read it as a sentence: "X is the spreadsheet of everything we know, n rows by p columns." That is all X is. The book's own advice is the same: visualise it as a spreadsheet. If you can point at a cell in a spreadsheet, you can read that display.

Two small add-ons, and then we are done with notation. A vector is just a list of numbers stacked in a column — a single row or a single column of that spreadsheet. And the superscript T means turn it on its side: a column becomes a row. The book uses that in a couple of displays and nowhere else.

The only formula on this page that does anything

Every now and then the book multiplies two grids together. The rule, translated: "to fill one box of the answer, take a row from the first grid and a column from the second, multiply the pairs one by one, and add them up." That is a weighted sum of a row — and you can check it on the book's own 2×2 example, where multiplying $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\3 & 4\end{pmatrix}$ by $\begin{pmatrix}5 & 6\\7 & 8\end{pmatrix}$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}19 & 22\\43 & 50\end{pmatrix}$, because $1\times5 + 2\times7 = 19$. The book uses this in a handful of places and skips it everywhere else — you can too.

6. How the book is organized

Chapter 2 is the vocabulary chapter — what f is, how we estimate it, and how we tell a good estimate from a bad one. Chapters 3 and 4 are the classical linear methods, for numbers and for categories respectively, and they carry most of the day-to-day work. Chapter 5 gives you the two tools for measuring accuracy honestly. Chapter 6 is about choosing which variables to trust. Everything after that is deliberately non-linear: splines and additive models (7), trees and boosting (8), support vector machines (9), neural networks (10), censored data (11), clustering with no answers at all (12), and finally the statistics of testing many hypotheses at once (13).

Every chapter ends with a Python lab, then exercises. The lab is where the method stops being a diagram and becomes a few lines of code on the book's own data.
