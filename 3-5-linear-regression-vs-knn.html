Comparison of linear regression with K-nearest neighbors

The chapter ends by handing the problem to the method that does not need any of this — no line, no coefficients, no p-values, just "average the similar points". It is the honest way to close: the whole apparatus of Sections 3.1–3.4 rests on an assumption about the shape of reality, and this section is where the book admits it and shows you what happens when you drop it.

Reading

  • Read Section 3.5 in full (pp. 111–116). Six pages, four figures, one argument. Figures 3.18 and 3.20 are the pair to sit with: one shows KNN losing, the other shows it losing again, for a completely different reason.
  • Skim The one-dimensional plots (Figures 3.17–3.19) if the pattern is obvious to you — curved truth, KNN wins; straight truth, linear regression wins.
  • Then Section 3.6 (the lab) fits all of this in Python on Boston housing and Carseats; the exercises come afterwards.

Key ideas

Parametric methods commit to a shape up front and estimate a handful of numbers — easy to fit, easy to interpret, wrong if the shape is wrong. Non-parametric methods commit to nothing and let the data draw the shape — no functional assumption, but they need far more data to be sure of anything. KNN regression is the simplest of those: predict the average y of the K nearest training points. Small K is wiggly (low bias, high variance); large K is smooth (high bias, low variance) — the bias-variance tradeoff from Chapter 2, drawn as a curve of test error against 1/K. And as the number of predictors grows, "nearest" stops meaning anything: the curse of dimensionality.

What you will know afterwards

  • parametric vs non-parametric, in one sentence each
  • KNN regression = average of the K neighbours
  • K = 1 interpolates; big K oversmooths
  • linear wins when the truth is linear
  • curse of dimensionality: p eats your n
  • when to accept worse MSE for explainability

1. Two ways to estimate a relationship

Everything in Sections 3.1–3.4 was parametric: we assumed the relationship was a line (or a plane, or a curve in the polynomial sense), then estimated the few numbers that pin the shape down. The book lists the trade plainly. Advantages: easy to fit because there are only a small number of coefficients to estimate, coefficients that have simple interpretations, and tests of statistical significance you can run with a t-table. Disadvantage: you assumed the form, so if the form is far from the truth and you care about prediction accuracy, the method performs poorly — and any conclusions drawn from it are suspect.

Non-parametric methods do not assume a form for f(X) at all. They give up the tidy coefficients and the p-values in exchange for being able to follow whatever shape the data actually has. The example in this section is the same idea that powered KNN classification in Chapter 2, applied to numbers instead of labels.

This is Chapter 2, revisited

You already met the bias-variance tradeoff when choosing K for a classifier. Nothing has changed except the average: instead of majority vote you take the mean, instead of an error rate you take a mean squared error. The flexibility dial, and the reason there is a best setting for it, are identical.

2. KNN regression: predict with your neighbours

Given a value K and the point you want to predict at, x₀: find the K training observations closest to x₀ — call that set N₀ — and average their responses.

$$\hat{f}(x_0) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{x_i \in N_0} y_i$$

No line is fitted and nothing is estimated before you ask. Figure 3.16 shows the shape this produces on two predictors: with K = 1 the fit passes exactly through every training point, which means it is a rough step function — a patchwork of flat tiles, each labelled by the single observation inside it. With K = 9 the tiles are still there (KNN always predicts a constant within a region) but they are averaged over nine points, so the surface is much smoother.

That is the whole tradeoff in one word: K. Small K is flexible — it can hug a curve exactly — but every prediction rests on one or two observations, so it inherits their noise and is wildly variable from sample to sample. Large K averages the noise away and gives a stable answer, but by averaging across a wide neighbourhood it can smooth over real structure. Low bias, high variance; then high bias, low variance.

3. So which one wins?

The book's answer is a rule with a condition, not a ranking: the parametric approach will outperform the non-parametric approach if the parametric form that has been selected is close to the true form of f(X).

  • Truth is linear (Figure 3.17–3.18). K = 1 predictions are far too variable and K = 9 is closer to f, but even K = 9 cannot beat a straight line: a non-parametric method incurs a cost in variance that is not offset by any reduction in bias, because there was no bias to remove. Linear regression's fit is "almost perfect" and its test MSE sits below KNN's across the board — though at very large K the gap narrows.
  • Truth bends slightly (Figure 3.19, top). A reversal at K ≥ 4: KNN starts to win, because it can chase the bend while the line is stuck being straight.
  • Truth bends a lot (Figure 3.19, bottom). KNN wins at every K. Notice which method's error actually moves as the relationship gets more curved: KNN's test MSE hardly changes at all, while linear regression's climbs steeply — its assumptions are what deteriorate, not its technique.
  • Extra useless predictors (Figure 3.20). And then the trap. Keep the same curved truth and add noise variables that have nothing to do with y: at p = 1 or 2 KNN wins, results are mixed at p = 3, and from p = 4 upward linear regression is better. Linear regression degrades slowly; KNN's error grows more than ten-fold. Fifty training observations describe a one-dimensional relationship generously and a twenty-dimensional one not at all — the K "nearest" points may be nowhere near x₀ in the high-dimensional sense. That is the curse of dimensionality: adding variables shrinks your effective sample size. The rule of thumb the section leaves you with: prefer parametric methods when there are few observations per predictor.

And even when KNN's test error is genuinely lower, there is a second criterion: interpretability. A linear model can be described in a sentence and comes with standard errors and p-values; KNN comes with a lookup table and a black box. If the difference in accuracy is small, the book's position is that giving up a little prediction accuracy for a model you can explain — and defend — is usually worth it.

Section 3.5 in one line

Linear regression assumes the shape and pays nothing when the assumption holds and dearly when it does not; KNN assumes nothing and pays in data — more neighbours smooth the variance, more dimensions destroy the neighbourhood, and when your sample is small relative to p, the assumption is the safer bet.
