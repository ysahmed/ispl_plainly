Other considerations in the regression model

Everything that can go wrong between "we fit a line" and "the line means something": categorical predictors, effects that depend on each other, curves, bad points, and predictors that answer each other's questions. The longest section of the chapter — and the most practical, because it is a list of the mistakes you will actually make.

Reading

  • Read All of 3.3.1 and 3.3.2 (pp. 91–100) — dummy variables and interactions are tools you will use every day. Then the six-item list in 3.3.3 (pp. 100–109), which is worth treating as a checklist rather than prose.
  • Skim The alternative codings of qualitative variables (the ±1 dummy, contrasts). The book flags them as "equivalent fits, different coefficients" and moves on; so should you, until you need a specific contrast.
  • Then Section 3.4 collects the chapter's seven questions and answers all of them in two pages.

Key ideas

A category becomes L − 1 dummy columns and one baseline; the fit is identical whichever level you baseline, only the reading changes. Multiplying two predictors together (an interaction) lets one effect depend on another — the TV × radio term lifts R² from 89.7% to 96.8%. Multiplying a predictor by itself (polynomial) bends the line while staying a linear model. And the diagnostic habit that catches all of it: plot the residuals and look for a pattern — a hill means non-linearity, a funnel means changing variance, runs mean missing variables, and one lone point far out in x can rotate the whole fit.

What you will know afterwards

  • dummy variables and the baseline level
  • why the coding choice cannot change the fit
  • interaction = slope that depends on another predictor
  • hierarchical principle
  • polynomial terms stay a linear model
  • reading a residual plot: hill, funnel, runs, lone point
  • outlier vs high leverage
  • collinearity, VIF, and the two fixes

1. When the predictor is a category (3.3.1)

The Credit data has four qualitative variables — own (house ownership), student, status and region — and no numbers anywhere in them. The trick is to invent numbers that carry no order: a dummy variable that is 1 for one level and 0 for everyone else.

$$x_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if person } i \text{ owns a house} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Fit balance ~ own and the model literally becomes two constants: everyone gets β̂₀, owners get an extra β̂₁. Table 3.7: intercept 509.80, own[Yes] 19.73 with a p-value of 0.67 — no evidence that owners carry more debt. Which raises the obvious worry: did coding owners as 1 rig the answer? No. Flip the coding and you get 529.53 and −19.73 — a different-looking pair that predicts exactly the same thing (509.80 + 19.73 = 529.53). Code with ±1 instead and the intercept becomes $519.665, the overall average, with ±$9.865 half the gap. The fit never changes; only the story you can tell about the coefficients does.

Three levels need two dummies, because you need a reference to measure from: region becomes I(South) and I(West), and everyone left over — the East — is the baseline. Table 3.8: baseline 531.00, South −12.50, West −18.69, both with p-values above 0.77. There is always one fewer dummy than levels, and the level you drop is your ruler. Since the choice of ruler is arbitrary, the book's advice when you care about the variable as a whole is to skip the individual p-values and run an F-test on the dummies together — p = 0.96 here, so region says nothing at all.

2. Letting the model bend: interactions and curves (3.3.2)

The standard model carries two strong assumptions. Additivity: the effect of TV does not depend on how much radio you bought. Linearity: every extra $1,000 of TV buys exactly the same uplift, whether it is your first or your hundredth. Both are frequently false, and both can be fixed without leaving the linear-model family.

The interaction term

Multiply two predictors and let the product in:

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 X_1 X_2 + \varepsilon$$

Rearrange it and the point drops out: the slope on X₁ is now β̃₁ = β₁ + β₃X₂ — a function of the other predictor. The book's factory example: an extra production line adds 3.4 units, plus 1.4 per worker, so lines matter more the more workers you have. For advertising (Table 3.9) the interaction coefficient is 0.0011 with t = 20.73, and R² jumps from 89.7% to 96.8% — the interaction alone soaks up 69% of the variance the additive model was leaving on the table. In words: $1,000 of TV is worth 19 + 1.1 × radio units, so TV and radio sell each other.

Two rules follow. The hierarchical principle: if the interaction stays, both main effects stay, even when their own p-values are unimpressive — X₁ × X₂ is correlated with X₁ and X₂, so dropping them changes what the interaction means. And interactions work with categories too, which is Figure 3.7 of the book: predict balance from income, once for students and once for everyone else.

Curves, without leaving linear regression

mpg against horsepower (Figure 3.8) is visibly bent. The fix looks like cheating and is not: add the square of the predictor as another column.

$$\mathrm{mpg} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times \mathrm{horsepower} + \beta_2 \times \mathrm{horsepower}^2 + \varepsilon$$

It is a curve in the data and still a straight line in the coefficients, so every tool of Sections 3.1–3.2 applies unchanged. The book's numbers (Table 3.10): 56.90, −0.4662 and +0.0012, with R² rising from 0.606 to 0.688 and the squared term significant at t = 10.1. Then the caution: extend to degree five and the fit "seems unnecessarily wiggly". More degree is not automatically better — the residual plot tells you when to stop.

3. Six ways it goes wrong (3.3.3)

The book's list, verbatim in structure: 1. non-linearity, 2. correlation of errors, 3. non-constant variance, 4. outliers, 5. high-leverage points, 6. collinearity. The first five are things you find by looking at the residuals — ideally a shapeless cloud with no pattern, no trend, constant spread and no lone outliers. The sixth is a thing you find by looking at the predictors. Two demos cover the catalogue:

Outliers (item 4) are points whose y is far from the line. Figure 3.12's observation 20 barely tilts the fit when removed — but it inflates the RSE from 0.77 to 1.09 and drags R² from 0.892 down to 0.805, and since RSE feeds every interval and p-value, one bad point degrades the whole report. The diagnostic is the studentized residual, a residual divided by its own estimated error: values beyond ±3 deserve a look. What does not have to be unusual for this to happen? The predictor. Which is the next item.

High-leverage points (item 5) are unusual in x — far from the rest of the data along the predictor axis, like Figure 3.13's observation 41. The leverage statistic makes it quantitative; for simple regression it is 1/n plus the squared distance of xi from x̄, so it grows with distance, always sits between 1/n and 1, and averages exactly (p + 1)/n — the rule of thumb is "much bigger than that and be suspicious". The dangerous combination is an outlier with leverage: unusual in y and far out in x, it can rotate the entire line. Add both points and compare:

4. Collinearity: predictors that answer for each other (3.3.3, item 6)

Collinearity is when two predictors move together so closely that the model cannot tell which one is doing the work. Figure 3.14 makes the contrast visual: age and limit show no relationship; rating and limit are almost the same variable twice. The consequence appears in Figure 3.15's contour plots — for unrelated predictors the RSS bowl has a tight, well-defined bottom, and for collinear ones it stretches into a long valley where a whole range of coefficient pairs fits equally well. Any nudge in the data can slide the answer anywhere along that valley, which in practice means inflated standard errors.

Table 3.11 shows the bill. Regress balance on age + limit and limit comes out 0.173 ± 0.005 (t = 34.5). Regress balance on rating + limit and limit becomes 0.025 ± 0.064 — a 12-fold jump in the standard error, p = 0.70, so a genuinely important variable now looks useless. Nothing about the data changed; only the neighbour.

Detecting it. Start with the correlation matrix of the predictors — any large value is a red flag. But three variables can be tangled up with no strong pair at all (multicollinearity), so the better tool is the variance inflation factor:

$$\mathrm{VIF}(\hat{\beta}_j) = \frac{1}{1 - R^2_{X_j \mid X_{-j}}}$$

Read it as: regress this predictor on all the others and see how well they explain it. If they reproduce it perfectly (R² → 1) you have learned nothing new by adding it, and the VIF explodes. The floor is 1 (no collinearity); anything above 5 or 10 is considered problematic. On Credit, balance ~ age + rating + limit gives VIFs of 1.01, 160.67, 160.59 — that is not a small problem.

Fixing it — two options. Drop the redundant variable: the book drops rating, VIFs fall back to ~1, and R² barely notices (0.754 → 0.750), because rating was repeating limit's information. Or combine them: average the standardised limit and rating into a single "credit worthiness" score and regress on that. Which you choose depends on whether you are trying to predict (either works) or to explain (you want the variable you can actually talk about).

Section 3.3 in one line

Categories become L − 1 dummy columns against a baseline; products between predictors let effects depend on each other; squares of predictors bend the curve while staying linear — and when any of it goes wrong, the residual plot tells you which of the six problems you have, while the correlation matrix and the VIF catch the one problem (collinearity) that hides in the predictors instead.
