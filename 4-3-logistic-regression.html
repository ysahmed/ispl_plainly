Logistic regression

The fix for 4.2's broken line: keep the straight-line score, but pass it through a curve that can only ever answer between 0 and 1. The coefficients stop being "units of Y" and become log-odds — a phrase that looks alarming and translates to one sentence: how much does this predictor multiply the odds of the event?

Reading

  • Read Section 4.3 in full (pp. 138–145). Subsections 4.3.1–4.3.3 are the core; 4.3.4's sign flip is the section's best lesson; 4.3.5 is the two-page extension to 3+ classes.
  • Skim The likelihood formula itself (4.5) — you need to recognise it as "multiply the fitted probabilities of what happened", not to reproduce it.
  • Then Section 4.4 introduces the other family — methods that model the predictors instead of the probability — and where each approach wins.

Key ideas

Logistic regression models the probability of a class as a sigmoid (S-shaped curve) of a straight-line score, so predictions can never leave [0, 1]. Coefficients are read as log-odds: a one-unit increase in X changes the log-odds by β, equivalently multiplies the odds by eβ — not the probability. Coefficients come from maximum likelihood (make the observed outcomes most probable), with z-statistics and p-values just like least squares. With several predictors, a coefficient's sign can flip versus the single-predictor model — correlation among predictors, not an error.

What you will know afterwards

  • the logistic curve p(X) = eᶻ / (1 + eᶻ)
  • odds = p / (1 − p)
  • logit: log-odds linear in X
  • e^β = odds multiplier per unit
  • maximum likelihood vs least squares
  • z-statistic, SE, p-value
  • dummy predictors
  • coefficient sign flips (confounding)
  • multinomial: one log-odds line per class

1. The logistic model (4.3.1)

We want to model p(X) = Pr(default = Yes | X), which must sit between 0 and 1 for every value of X. The straight line of (4.1), p(X) = β₀ + β₁X, cannot promise that — as Figure 4.2 showed, it dips below zero for small balances and would exceed 1 for large ones. So logistic regression squashes the straight line with the logistic function:

$$p(X) = \frac{e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 X}}{1 + e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 X}}$$

Read it as: "score the person with a straight line, then run the score through the S-curve." No matter how negative or huge the score, the output is squeezed into (0, 1); near the middle the curve is steep, at the ends it flattens. The book notes a sanity check: the average fitted probability comes out 0.0333 either way — exactly the overall default rate in the data, as it must.

Odds, and why the log of them

Algebra on the formula gives the odds:

$$\frac{p(X)}{1 - p(X)} = e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 X}$$

Odds are "how likely versus not", the language of the betting track — the book's examples: odds of 1/4 means 1 in 5 people default (p = 0.2), odds of 9 means 9 in 10 do (p = 0.9). Odds run from 0 to ∞ — no ceiling at 1, which is exactly why the exponent can be a plain straight line. Take the log of both sides and you get the form the model actually uses:

$$\log\left(\frac{p(X)}{1 - p(X)}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$$

This is the logit equation: the log-odds is linear in X, even though the probability is not. Two properties of β₁ follow, and the book stresses the second:

  • β₁ is a change in log-odds, so eβ₁ is a multiplier on the odds. In the fitted Default model, a $1 increase in balance has β₁ = 0.0055, so a $100 increase multiplies the odds of default by e0.55 ≈ 1.73 — each extra hundred dollars makes default about 73% more likely in odds terms.
  • It is not a change in the probability. Adding the same amount to the probability of someone near 0.5 and someone near 0.01 is impossible — the S-curve flattens. What survives is the direction: positive β₁ always means higher X ⇒ higher probability of class 1, negative always the reverse.

2. Estimating the coefficients (4.3.2)

How do the β̂ get chosen? Not by least squares — by maximum likelihood: pick the coefficients that make the observed data most probable under the model. Concretely, the likelihood is a product over people:

$$\ell(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \prod_{i:\,y_i = 1} p(x_i) \;\times \prod_{i:\,y_i = 0} \bigl(1 - p(x_i)\bigr)$$

In words: "for everyone who defaulted, multiply in their fitted probability of default; for everyone who didn't, multiply in their fitted probability of no default; then find the β that makes the total as big as possible." Push that product toward 1 and you have fitted the model well. The book adds that least squares itself is a special case of maximum likelihood — the same principle, different distribution assumed.

Everything that follows least squares then carries over: each coefficient gets a standard error, and the ratio

$$z = \hat{\beta} / \mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta})$$

(the z-statistic, the logistic analogue of regression's t-statistic) tests β against zero, with a p-value to read off. Fitting default on balance alone gives the book's Table 4.1:

Read the balance row the 4.3.1 way: each extra dollar of balance adds 0.0055 to the log-odds of default — an enormous z of 24.9, i.e. balance is unambiguously associated with default. The intercept's job, as in Chapter 3, is to put the average fitted probability back where the data says it is: 3.33%.

Why the coefficient is so tiny and so huge

0.0055 looks negligible until you remember it lives in log-odds: over a $1,000 range of balance it accumulates 5.5 log-odds units, a factor of e5.5 ≈ 245 in the odds. Never judge a logistic coefficient before you decide what a "unit" means — in Section 4.6 the book will suggest reporting factors (eβ) instead.

3. Making predictions (4.3.3)

With coefficients in hand, prediction is substitution. The book's two worked examples from Table 4.1:

  • balance = $1,000 → p̂ = e−10.6513+0.0055×1000 / (1 + …) = 0.00576 — under a 1% chance of default.
  • balance = $2,000 → p̂ = 0.586 — 58.6%: the same person-plus-$1,000, transformed from near-certain to more-likely-than-not by the S-curve's steep middle.

Both are below (or above) thresholds you might choose: predict default when p̂ > 0.5 by default, or a conservative p̂ > 0.1 if missing a defaulter hurts more than a false alarm. Here is the actual fit — drag the balance to any value and read the model's verdict:

Predictors can be categories too, via the dummy trick from Section 3.3.1. Fit default on student status alone and Table 4.2 gives intercept −3.5041 and student[Yes] +0.4049 (p = 0.0004) — students look riskier:

  • Pr(default | student) = 0.0431 — 4.3%
  • Pr(default | non-student) = 0.0292 — 2.9%

4. Multiple logistic regression, and a sign that flips (4.3.4)

Several predictors? Just as in Chapter 3, extend the score:

$$\log\left(\frac{p(X)}{1 - p(X)}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p$$

Fit default on balance, income and student status together — Table 4.3:

CoefficientStd. errorz-statisticp-value
Intercept  −10.86900.4923−22.08<0.0001
balance  0.00570.000224.74<0.0001
income  0.00300.00820.370.7115
student[Yes]  −0.64680.2362−2.740.0062

Two surprises on one page:

  • Income does nothing once balance is in the model (p = 0.72). Whatever income carried, balance already carries it better.
  • The student sign flipped. Alone, being a student raised default risk (+0.4049, Table 4.2); holding balance fixed, being a student lowers it (−0.6468, Table 4.3) — yet both are statistically significant. Is one of them wrong?

No — the coefficients answer different questions. Table 4.2 asks "are students more likely to default, on average?" (yes: 4.3% vs 2.9%). Table 4.3 asks "among people with the same balance, are students safer?" (yes). Both are true because students tend to carry more balance — students are riskier as a group and safer at matched balance. The book illustrates it with Figure 4.3: split the default rate by balance for students and non-students (the curves sit close together) and compare the marginal averages (students higher) — the difference lives in the balance distribution, not in the student label.

The lesson to keep

A coefficient's sign is a statement about a conditional comparison — "holding the other predictors fixed". Add or remove a predictor and you change the question being answered. If a sign flips between two models, both models can be right; go look at how the predictors move together (this is the same machinery as Chapter 3's confounding, and it will return in exercises throughout the book).

5. More than two classes: multinomial logistic (4.3.5)

Binary model handles two classes; the emergency room has three. Multinomial logistic regression generalises it: pick one class as the baseline (say, stroke) and write one log-odds equation comparing every other class to it:

$$\log\left(\frac{\Pr(Y = k \mid X)}{\Pr(Y = K \mid X)}\right) = \beta_{k0} + \beta_{k1}X_1 + \cdots + \beta_{kp}X_p, \quad k = 1, \ldots, K-1$$

For three conditions you get two straight lines of log-odds — "overdose versus stroke" and "seizure versus stroke". Prediction: compute all K fitted probabilities and assign the largest. Two things worth knowing:

  • The baseline is a bookkeeping choice. Whichever class you pick, the final predictions are identical — only the meaning of the printed coefficients changes. Pick the class you want to compare everything against.
  • The softmax coding avoids a baseline entirely. Equation (4.13) gives every class its own parameters symmetrically, with the probabilities written as eβk·X normalised across classes so they sum to 1. The book notes this form appears again in Chapter 10's neural networks — good to recognise it now.

Section 4.3 in one line

Model the probability itself: score people with a straight line, squash it through the S-curve, fit by maximum likelihood, read coefficients as odds multipliers (eβ), expect z-tests like before — and remember that with several predictors each coefficient answers a "holding the others fixed" question, which is why the student sign flips when balance enters the model. Section 4.4 turns the problem inside out instead: model the predictors.
