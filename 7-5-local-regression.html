Local regression

Every method so far committed to one function — a polynomial, a set of bins, a spline, a smoothing curve — and then evaluated it anywhere you liked. This section gives that up. To estimate the fit at a target point, gather the observations around that point, fit them a small weighted regression, and read off the value at the target. The weights change with every target, so the model is rebuilt on the spot each time — the book calls it a memory-based procedure, and the only knob that really matters is how much of the data counts as "nearby".

Reading

  • Read Section 7.6 in full (pp. 303–305). One figure of simulated data (Figure 7.9) to fix the picture, Algorithm 7.1 in four steps with one numbered equation (7.14), a short paragraph on the three choices the method leaves open, Figure 7.10 with two fits to the Wage data, and a closing paragraph on generalising to several variables — where local regression starts to fail.
  • Skim Algorithm 7.1 itself, until each of its four steps is a sentence you could say aloud, and the paragraph after it explaining why a new target point means a brand-new weighted least squares fit rather than a lookup in an old one.
  • Then Section 7.7, which stops choosing between these one-predictor methods and adds several of them together into one model.

Key ideas

To estimate at $x_0$: take the closest $s \cdot n$ training points (Algorithm 7.1, step 1), weight them so the weight falls smoothly to zero at the edge of that neighbourhood (step 2 — the yellow bell in Figure 7.9), minimise the weighted squared error of a constant, linear or quadratic (step 3; equation (7.14) is the linear one), and report the fitted value at $x_0$ (step 4: $\hat f(x_0) = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_0$). Because the weights are tied to $x_0$, they are different for every target: predicting one new point costs one new weighted regression over all the training data you kept — that is what memory-based means. The span $s$ is this section's tuning parameter, playing the role $\lambda$ played in section 7.5: small $s$ follows every wiggle, large $s$ drifts back toward a global fit, and cross-validation can pick it. Two counts of how flexible the answer was are printed on this page — $\mathrm{tr}(S)$ along the smoother's diagonal and $\mathrm{tr}(S^\top S)$ of its squared row lengths — and the book's Figure 7.10 legend quotes a third, which this page tracks down to R's own output (16.41607 and 5.29968 for the two spans).

1. A regression per point

The section opens by naming its difference from everything before it: local regression "involves computing the fit at a target point $x_0$ using only the nearby training observations." Sections 7.1 through 7.5 each built a single curve — degree-4 polynomial, four bins, three knots, one $\lambda$ — and could then be evaluated anywhere for free. This section never builds that curve. Ask for the fit at age 47 and it assembles a regression out of the men near 47, fits it, reads off its value at 47, and lets the regression go; ask at 48, and the neighbourhood — and therefore the weights, and therefore the model — is a different one.

Figure 7.9 is the picture version, and the book walks through its parts: the blue curve is the true $f(x)$ the data came from, "the light orange line corresponds to the local regression estimate $\hat f(x)$", the orange points are the ones "local to the target point", the orange vertical line marks $x_0$, and "the yellow bell-shape superimposed on the plot indicates weights assigned to each point, decreasing to zero with distance from the target point". The book describes its two targets as "one target point near 0.4, and another near the boundary at 0.05"; measured against the printed page's axis ticks, the two vertical lines sit at $x_0 = 0.4562$ and $x_0 = 0.0649$ — exactly where the demo below starts.

Algorithm 7.1 — Local regression at $X = x_0$

  1. Gather the fraction $s = k/n$ of training points whose $x_i$ are closest to $x_0$.
  2. Assign each a weight $K_{i0} = K(x_i, x_0)$ "so that the point furthest from $x_0$ has weight zero, and the closest has the highest weight. All but these $k$ nearest neighbors get weight zero."
  3. Fit a weighted least squares of the $y_i$ on the $x_i$ with those weights, minimising equation (7.14) below.
  4. The fitted value at $x_0$: $\hat f(x_0) = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x_0$.
$$\min_{\beta_0,\,\beta_1}\;\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} K_{i0}\,\bigl(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i\bigr)^2 \qquad(7.14)$$

Nothing here is new machinery: step 3 is ordinary least squares with different weights on the rows — the same normal equations as chapter 3, evaluated on $k$ rows instead of $n$. What is new is the book's observation right after the algorithm: the $K_{i0}$ differ "for each value of $x_0$", so "in order to obtain the local regression fit at a new point, we need to fit a new weighted least squares regression model by minimizing (7.14) for a new set of weights". That is why the method keeps the whole training set around — "local regression is sometimes referred to as a memory-based procedure, because like nearest-neighbors, we need all the training data each time we wish to compute a prediction." Compare that with the smoothing spline of the last section, which compressed all 3,000 rows into one matrix and one $\lambda$ and never looked back.

Three choices are open, says the book: "how to define the weighting function $K$", and "whether to fit a linear, constant, or quadratic regression in Step 3", with the span $s$ coming later as "the most important" one. This page fixes $K$ as the tricube — the bell every implementation of local regression draws, and the one printed in Figure 7.9:

$$K(d) = \begin{cases} (1 - d^3)^3 & d < 1 \\[2pt] 0 & d \ge 1 \end{cases}$$

where $d$ is distance from the target in units of the bandwidth $h$. That claim — that the printed yellow bell is this function — is measured, not assumed: fit the tricube profile to the bell in each of the two printed panels and you get half-widths 0.1985 and 0.2010 with peaks 0.996 and 0.970, an rmse of 0.009 and 0.029 against the printed pixels. A peak at 1.0 and a smooth fall to zero at the edge, both panels — the tricube. The default degree here is 1, because equation (7.14) — the one the book numbers — is the linear one.

At the opening position — target A, $x_0 = 0.0649$, span 0.2, degree 1 — the neighbourhood holds 20 of the 100 points with bandwidth h = 0.1973, and the estimate reads f̂(x₀) = 0.2362 against the truth f(x₀) = 0.2560: an error of −0.0198. Press target B ($x_0 = 0.4562$) and the bandwidth roughly halves, to 0.1014 — at an interior target the 20 nearest points come from both sides, at the boundary they can only come from the right — while the error grows to +0.0889. Switch to the quadratic and the error at target A grows to −0.1019: twice the parameters, the same 20 points, more variance. Open the span to 0.7 instead ($k = 70$, $h = 0.6710$) and the error shrinks to +0.0240 — more points averaging the noise, at the price of a line that follows the curve less closely. That is the trade the span dial makes, live.

2. The span, and whose degrees of freedom

The book's verdict on the three choices is blunt: "While all of these choices make some difference, the most important choice is the span $s$." Its analogy is exact and worth keeping: "The span plays a role like that of the tuning parameter $\lambda$ in smoothing splines: it controls the flexibility of the non-linear fit. The smaller the value of $s$, the more local and wiggly will be our fit; alternatively, a very large value of $s$ will lead to a global fit to the data using all of the training observations." And then: "We can again use cross-validation to choose $s$, or we can specify it directly."

Specifying it directly is what Figure 7.10 does — two fits, spans 0.7 and 0.2, with the book's flat observation: "As expected, the fit obtained using $s = 0.7$ is smoother than that obtained using $s = 0.2$." The demo below draws both of the book's spans as grey reference curves behind whichever span you choose, plus something the printed figure only hints at in its legend: the counts.

First, $k = s \cdot n$ is a count of people, and people's ages are integers — so the neighbourhood is bigger than the recipe asks for. At span 0.2 the recipe wants the closest 600 of 3,000 men to age 47; the 600th lands at $h = 3$ years, and every man from 44 to 50 is tied at or inside that distance: the readout says 637 within h, not 600. Keeping the tied extras is this page's convention (they arrive with tricube weight from positive down to exactly zero at $d = h$, which is what step 2 prescribes for the outermost ring anyway); the book's step 1 says "the fraction $s = k/n$ of training points whose $x_i$ are closest" and does not say what to do when "closest" runs through a tie.

Second, "degrees of freedom" in the printed legend is a number with a third convention. At the defaults — span 0.2, degree 1 — this page counts df = tr(S) = 10.64 and enp = tr(S′S) = 8.92, RSS 4,754,977. At span 0.7: 3.69 and 3.23, RSS 4,789,317. At 0.5, the span the lab's lowess uses: 4.90 and 4.22. All six counts shrink as the span grows — a wider window is a blunter instrument — but none of them is 16.4. So where does the legend's pair, "16.4 Degrees of Freedom" and "5.3 Degrees of Freedom", come from? Not from the local-linear fits drawn beside them. R's loess() fits a local quadratic by default, and asking it directly (this page runs R on every check, when docker is available) gives enp = 16.41607 at span 0.2 and 5.29968 at span 0.7 — the book's 16.4 and 5.3, to every digit printed. Our own quadratic counts at span 0.2 are tr(S) = 18.28 and tr(S′S) = 16.57: the first agrees with R's degree-2 surface evaluation to eight digits, the second is close to 16.41607 but not equal, because R reports its enp on the interpolated grid it predicts from while this page counts at the 3,000 observed ages. Three defensible counts of slightly different smoothers, all printed side by side above — the honest move, rather than quoting 16.4 as the degrees of freedom of the line you are looking at.

One edge the slider exposes, because with tied integer data the tricube can bite: drag the span to its minimum, 0.05, and at age 47 the bandwidth falls to 1 year — the 150-nearest to 47 all sit at 46, 47 or 48 (271 men). The men at exactly 47 get weight 1, the men one year away get $(1 - 1^3)^3 = 0$, and a weighted "line" through a single $x$-value has no slope left to estimate: the fit collapses to that age's plain mean. The readouts report the mixture honestly — df = 39.87, between the 61 you would get if every age collapsed to its mean and the small numbers a real line fit would give. A span that small is not on CV's shortlist anyway, as the next demo shows.

3. Cross-validation, and how flat it is

"We can again use cross-validation to choose $s$." The word again is doing work: this is the third time this chapter picks a tuning parameter by holding data out — degree for polynomials in 7.1, the knot count in 7.4, $\lambda$ in 7.5 — and by now you know what to expect from the curve it draws. The demo runs the book's own recipe faithfully: ten folds, the same fixed fold assignment the first CV demo on this site used (seed 7, so the numbers are reproducible from the page source), twenty spans from 0.05 to 1.00, one local-linear fit — equation (7.14), degree 1 — per held-out man per span. That is 60,000 weighted regressions per sweep, done in your browser.

The answer, today: at span 0.20 the criterion reads 1592.1 ± 67.9, and the minimum of all twenty spans sits at 0.25, at 1591.5 — six-tenths of a unit below, against a standard error of nearly sixty-eight. Across the entire sweep, from span 0.05 to 1.00, the curve moves by a total of 46.0 units: every span on the grid is within less than one standard error of the best. Click reshuffle — the fold seed walks from 7 to 1282168119 — and the minimum moves to 0.30, at 1594.1, while span 0.20 now reads 1595.0 ± 63.3. The argmin is not a fact; the plateau is. So the honest reading of "we can use cross-validation to choose $s$" here is: it rules out the extremes, prefers the 0.2–0.4 neighbourhood, and declines to be more precise than the noise allows — which is exactly how flat the leave-one-out curve in the previous section turned out to be, for the same reason: near its minimum, a criterion for flexibility is almost always a long, shallow valley, and this chapter's method should be chosen from the valley floor, not from the argmin pin. The lab that follows uses spans 0.2 and 0.5 — both inside the valley — and we will get there on the next page.

The section closes by pointing outward: local regression generalises "in a setting with multiple features" to varying coefficient models — "global in some variables, but local in another, such as time" — and naturally to two-dimensional neighbourhoods. It also states its own failure mode, which is the curse of dimensionality you met with $k$-nearest neighbors in chapter 3: "local regression can perform poorly if $p$ is much larger than about 3 or 4 because there will generally be very few training observations close to $x_0$." Beyond a handful of dimensions, a neighbourhood is empty, and no amount of weighting helps.

Section 7.6 in one line

Stop fitting one curve to everything: at each target $x_0$, take the $s \cdot n$ nearest points, weight them with a bell that reaches zero at the edge, fit a small weighted polynomial, and read its value at $x_0$ — refitted for every target, which is why the method is memory-based; the span is the dial (cross-validation can turn it, though here it hands you a plateau), the counts of flexibility depend on whose convention you ask — ours print 10.64 and 8.92 where the book's legend prints 16.4, a number this page traces to R's default quadratic enp, 16.41607, measured directly.
