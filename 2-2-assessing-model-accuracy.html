Assessing model accuracy

No method is best for every dataset — the book says so outright — so the real question is always “how good is this method on this data?”. Answering it needs a number (mean squared error), an honest place to compute it (data you did not train on), and one piece of theory that explains why the answer has a U shape.

Reading

  • Read All of 2.2 — it is short (fourteen pages) and it is the most reusable section in the chapter. Figures 2.9 to 2.11 and Figure 2.17 are the ones people come back to.
  • Slow down on 2.2.2, the bias–variance trade-off. Everything about overfitting for the rest of the book is a restatement of it.
  • Reappears in Chapter 5 (cross-validation — actually estimating test error when you have no test data) and Chapter 6 (choosing the model with the best estimate).

Key ideas

Score a model by the average squared miss on data it never trained on — the test MSE. Training error is easy to compute and misleadingly low: methods are built to minimise it, so it only ever falls as you add flexibility, while test error falls, bottoms out, then climbs (the U shape). The U exists because test error breaks into three stacked pieces — irreducible noise, bias (too stiff), variance (too twitchy) — and flexibility pushes bias down while pushing variance up. In classification the same story is told by error rates, with the Bayes error rate as the floor no classifier can pass.

1. No free lunch: why a ruler is needed

Section 2.2 opens with the reason this section exists. The book plans to teach you many methods, not one, because no method wins everywhere: the best performer on one dataset may be mediocre on the next, similar-but-different dataset. So for every analysis you have to decide which method is working best here — and that decision needs a measurement, not an opinion.

The practical consequence

You will never get to see the answer key. Almost all real analyses run on data with no held-back test set sitting in a drawer, which is why the last line of this section points at cross-validation (Chapter 5): estimating the test error from the training data. Understand the target first, then the estimate.

2. Measuring the quality of fit (2.2.1)

In the regression setting the standard score is the mean squared error: predict, subtract the truth, square the miss, do it for every row, average.

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - \hat{f}(x_i)\bigr)^2$$

Read it as a sentence: "for each of the n rows, take the answer we gave minus the answer that happened, square it so misses always count positive and big misses count much more, then average." Squaring does two jobs: it kills the minus signs (a miss of −5 and a miss of +5 are equally bad) and it punishes a few large misses more than many small ones.

Two MSEs, and only one of them is honest

Compute the MSE on the rows you trained with and you get the training MSE — cheap, available, and the wrong question. What you actually care about is a row you have never seen: a new customer, a next day, a different patient. The book writes that as the test MSE, the average squared miss on fresh observations (x₀, y₀).

The trap: methods choose their coefficients to minimise the training MSE. So training error can always be driven down — give a model enough freedom and it will pass through your data almost exactly — while its behaviour on new rows gets worse and worse. Three details the book is careful about:

  • Training MSE is almost always lower than test MSE, whether or not you have overfit — methods are built to minimise the first.
  • Overfitting has a precise meaning: not “the test error is high”, but “a less flexible model would have had a smaller test error”. The word names the wrong choice, not the bad result.
  • The best flexibility moves around. Where the bottom of the U sits depends on the dataset — sometimes at “a straight line”, sometimes far to the right. Which is why you need a way to find it, not a favourite setting.

The easy part and the hard part

Training MSE: you can always compute it. Test MSE: you usually cannot, because you have no test data. The rest of this book spends a lot of energy on that gap — Chapter 5's cross-validation splits your data into pretend-train and pretend-test over and over to get an estimate without ever touching outside data.

3. The bias–variance trade-off (2.2.2)

Why would test error ever go up as a model gets smarter? Because it is not one error but three, stacked — and they do not move together:

$$\text{test MSE} = \underbrace{\text{Bias}^2}_{\text{too stiff}} + \underbrace{\text{Variance}}_{\text{too twitchy}} + \underbrace{\text{Var}(\varepsilon)}_{\text{fixed}}$$

Read as: "the error you will see on new data equals how badly the method's assumed shape misses reality, plus how much the fit jumps around when you redraw the data, plus the noise floor you can never beat."

Bias² Systematic wrongness. How far the average fit sits from the true f, over and over. A straight line fitted to a curved world has high bias: it is wrong the same way every time. More flexibility → less bias.
Variance Twitchiness. How much the fit changes if you redraw the training data and run the method again. A model that treats noise as pattern swings wildly from sample to sample. More flexibility → more variance.
Var(ε) The floor. The irreducible noise from Section 1. It does not care what you do, so it is a flat line under every chart.

How to read the three verdicts:

  • Too stiff — bias dominates. The model cannot follow the shape; every redraw gives the same wrong answer, so variance is small and bias² is the whole error. Raise flexibility.
  • Too twitchy — variance dominates. The model is fitting the noise; each redraw gives a different answer, so variance swamps bias². Lower flexibility.
  • Near the balance point. The bottom of the U — every other degree scores a higher test error: one step down would trade variance back for bias, one step up buys variance for nothing.

The one sentence to remember

Adding flexibility always reduces bias and always increases variance; test MSE is their sum plus a constant, so somewhere in between is a minimum — and where that minimum sits is different for every dataset. Finding it is the job of Chapter 5.

4. The classification setting (2.2.3)

When the response is a category, the score is the error rate: the fraction of rows you labelled wrongly. Everything else carries over — training error is on the rows you fitted, test error is on rows you did not, flexibility still produces a U shape — but classification adds two ideas of its own.

The Bayes classifier, and the floor it establishes

Suppose you knew the true probabilities: at every possible input x, the chance of each class. The best possible rule would then be obvious — pick the most likely class. That rule has a name, the Bayes classifier, and where the two class probabilities are exactly equal you are standing on the Bayes decision boundary.

Even the Bayes classifier makes mistakes, because the classes genuinely overlap: some people with identical measurements end up in different classes. How often it is wrong is the Bayes error rate — the classification twin of Var(ε). No classifier, with any amount of data, gets under it:

KNN as the practical approximation

Nobody has the true probabilities, so the KNN classifier estimates them from data instead: find the K training rows nearest to the query and take their majority class (or their class fraction, if you want a probability). Small K hugs the data; large K smooths towards the overall class frequency. K is the flexibility knob again — and it behaves exactly as Section 3 promised:

  • K = 1 gives zero training error (each point is its own nearest neighbour) and a boundary so wiggly that fresh points land on the wrong side. Perfect homework, poor predictions — the classification version of overfitting.
  • K very large is one majority vote over nearly the whole dataset: a boundary that is nearly a straight line, indifferent to the actual shape. Underfitting.
  • Somewhere in between the two curves come closest to the floor. Note that neither curve reaches it: the gap that remains is the irreducible overlap of the two clouds.

Section 2.2 in one line

Score models on data they have not seen; expect training error to lie to you; expect test error to form a U as flexibility grows, because bias falls while variance rises; and remember that in classification the Bayes error rate is a hard floor — the goal is to get close to it, not to reach zero.
