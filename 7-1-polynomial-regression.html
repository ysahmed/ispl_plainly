Polynomial regression

Chapters 3 to 6 argued about which predictors to put in a model that they all assumed was straight. Section 7.1 changes the question: keep age, and let the fitted curve bend. The move is old and it is almost embarrassingly simple — put the powers of the predictor into the model alongside it — but it has a consequence that shapes everything else in this chapter. The fit is still a linear model in its unknowns, so every tool from Chapter 3 still works, and yet the thing you actually look at is no longer a coefficient. It is a curve.

Reading

  • Read Section 7.1 in full (pp. 291–292). It is two pages and it sets the pattern for the rest of the chapter: change the basis, keep the linear-model machinery, look at the fitted function.
  • Skim The algebra of equation (7.2) and its footnote. The idea underneath it is what matters — a fitted value at one chosen x is a known linear combination of the coefficients, so its variance is that combination run through the coefficient covariance matrix. You will use the same argument for splines in a few pages without ever doing it by hand.
  • Then Section 7.2, which keeps the flexibility but throws away the global structure. A polynomial has to obey itself everywhere at once; step functions do not.

Key ideas

A polynomial in one predictor is still a linear model. The columns are $x, x^2, \ldots, x^d$, the unknowns are the coefficients, and least squares, standard errors, the t and F distributions all apply without a single change. What changes is what you read off the fit: past d = 2 the individual coefficients stop meaning much on their own, so the object of interest becomes the fitted function across a grid of x. A pointwise band around that function is the same argument again — $\hat f(x_0)$ is a known linear combination of the coefficients, so its variance is eq. (7.2) run through $\hat C$. And because a degree-d polynomial can bend anywhere, the book stops at d = 4 for wage against age: higher degrees chase noise, especially at the ends of the range where the data are thinnest.

What you will know afterwards

  • why a polynomial in x is a linear model in disguise
  • why the coefficients stop being interesting past d = 2
  • that you read a fitted function, not a table
  • where the pointwise 2× standard error band comes from
  • that 79 high earners in 3,000 rows means a wide band
  • that training error always votes for a higher degree
  • that cross-validation cannot tell four from nine here

1. The curve the coefficients make

Write the straight line out with its powers added:

$$y_i \;=\; \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 x_i^2 + \beta_3 x_i^3 + \cdots + \beta_d x_i^d + \varepsilon_i \qquad(7.1)$$

That is a standard linear model whose predictors happen to be the first d powers of x, so least squares estimates the betas exactly as it always did. The book notes that d above 3 or 4 is unusual, for a reason that has nothing to do with the algebra: a high-degree polynomial is flexible everywhere at once, and flexibility everywhere means it can take on strange shapes — most visibly near the ends of the data, where a polynomial is forced to keep growing or shrinking according to a rule it inherited from the middle of the range.

The Wage data behind every figure in this chapter is the book's own: 3,000 men in the middle Atlantic states, surveyed across seven years, ages 18 to 80, with wage recorded in thousands of dollars. Because it comes from the book's package rather than a simulation, we can check our arithmetic against the book's own printed output. The lab in section 7.8 fits exactly the model this demo fits — wage on a fourth-degree polynomial of age — and prints the coefficients. Ours are the same numbers: an intercept of 111.7036, then 447.0679, −478.3158, 125.5217 and −77.9112, with standard errors of 0.729 on the intercept and 39.915 on each of the four terms. You will not usually care about those five numbers, and the book says as much — they are quoted here only because they tell you the fit on your screen is the fit in the book.

The coefficients are not the answer

The readout prints the fit's five numbers at every degree, and at degree four they are the book's own lab table to the last digit. But no amount of staring at 447.07 and −478.32 tells you what wage does with age, because those two numbers only make sense together with the other three, evaluated at a particular age. The book's point is that with a polynomial you look at the function — the curve across the range — and not at the table. That is why every figure in this chapter plots a fitted curve over a grid of x values rather than reporting a coefficient.

2. A yes-or-no response

Looking at the wage scatter, something stands out: a small group of men earn far more than everyone else, above $250,000 a year, and they sit apart from the bulk of the data. The book's move is to stop treating that as an awkward tail and start treating it as a question. Split the response at $250,000 and ask a different one: given a man's age, how likely is he to be in that group?

$$\Pr(y_i > 250 \mid x_i) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\big(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \beta_2 x_i^2 + \cdots + \beta_d x_i^d\big)}{1 + \exp\!\big(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \cdots + \beta_d x_i^d\big)} \qquad(7.3)$$

Equation (7.3) is logistic regression — the whole of section 4.3 — with the polynomial doing exactly the job the single term in x did before. The coefficients are still fitted by maximum likelihood, and the fitted curve is still read off a grid of x values. What is different is that we are now estimating a probability, and a probability built from a small number of events.

How small is worth stating plainly: of the 3,000 men, 79 earn more than $250,000. The book draws attention to this and it is the reason its right-hand panel has such a wide interval. Seven events per year of age is not much to estimate a curve from.

A wide interval is information, not a defect

The band on the left panel is wide because the evidence is thin, and the honest response is to say so rather than to reach for a method that returns a narrower one. At its widest — age 67 — the interval runs from about −1% to 6.5%, nearly two-fifths of a panel whose axis only reaches 20%, and its lower end goes below zero, which a probability cannot be. The curve is drawn clipped at the axis while the readout prints the fit's own unclamped numbers, because the model is telling you that on this data, age alone does not pin down the probability of being a very high earner. Nothing in section 4 would change that; the width comes from having 79 events and five parameters.

3. How far up should the degree go?

So far the degree has been a choice we made because the book made it. What should decide it? The obvious candidate — the one chapter 6 spent a whole section disqualifying — is the training error, and it is worth watching what it does here. Adding a power can never make the training residual sum of squares worse, because the degree-3 model sits inside the degree-4 model and the extra coefficient can always be set to zero. So the training mean squared error falls at every step: 1,674.1 at degree 1, 1,590.5 at degree 4, 1,585.6 at degree 10. It never turns a corner and it never says stop. The cross-validated curve does both. On this data the two are close — 3,000 rows carrying ten parameters is not much overfitting — but they are close in one direction only, and the gap between them, 1.4 units at degree 1 and 6.7 at degree 10, is the optimism that training error is hiding.

The cross-validation answer is subtler than the textbook version suggests, and the demo below is built so you can see why. Ten-fold cross-validation with a fixed shuffle gives a curve that drops steeply from degree 1 to degree 3 and is then flat — flat in a specific, measurable sense. From degree 3 to degree 10 the cross-validated mean squared error stays within about three units of 1,592, while the standard error of any single point on that curve is about 67 — more than twenty times the entire spread of the curve above degree 3, so the data cannot tell those models apart. On the shuffle this page starts with, the minimum lands on degree 9; press the button to reshuffle the ten folds and it moves — ours has landed on 4, 6 and 9 on different draws. That is not the method failing, it is the method reporting that there is nothing here to choose between. What the curve really says is only two things: degree 1, a straight line, is too small, and everything from degree 3 upwards is one model for practical purposes. The book's lab stops at four, and four is as defensible an answer as any the curve offers.

Section 7.1 in one line

Put the powers of a predictor into a linear model and you have a flexible curve with none of the machinery changed: least squares still applies, the standard errors still apply, and a pointwise band is a linear combination of the coefficient covariance. What changes is what you read — a fitted function across a grid, not a coefficient — and how you choose the flexibility, which has to come from something that estimates test error rather than training error, because training error will always vote for the highest degree on offer.

Source: ISLP, Section 7.1 (pp. 291–292). Every curve, band and cross-validation point on this page is fitted in your browser from the book's own Wage data set — 3,000 observations, ages 18 to 80, wage in thousands of dollars, 79 of them above 250 — as shipped with the book's Python package. The fourth-degree coefficients and their standard errors are the book's own lab table for section 7.8.1, reproduced by the fit above rather than quoted from the page. The orthogonal polynomial columns used throughout are the ones R's poly() and ISLP's Poly() build, so the fitted curves and bands are the functions the book plots, even though the intermediate coefficients are expressed in that rotated basis rather than in raw powers.
