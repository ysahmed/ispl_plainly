Considerations in high dimensions

Sections 6.1 to 6.3 all assumed there are more rows than predictors. Section 6.4 takes that away, and the ground goes fast: once the number of features reaches the number of observations, least squares stops being a method that overfits and becomes a method with no unique answer at all. The two demos below are the book's Figures 6.23 and 6.24 rebuilt on data of our own — one turns a model made entirely of noise features into a perfect fit, the other shows what cross-validation does about it when 2,000 features stand behind 100 rows.

Reading

  • Read Section 6.4 in full (pp. 263–267). It is the shortest section in the chapter and the only one that is mostly about what you must not do: four subsections, a definition, two figures and a list of the words that stop being meaningful.
  • Skim The captions of Figures 6.23 and 6.24. Each one is the whole argument in three sentences; the pages that follow unpack what the picture already shows.
  • Then Equation (6.2) from section 6.1.3 once more. The σ̂² that Cp, AIC and BIC all divide by is exactly the quantity 6.4.2 says you cannot estimate here, so it is worth knowing where it came from before you are told it is gone.

Key ideas

Everything so far took p < n as given; 6.4 takes it away and shows the failure is not a gradual worsening. At p = n the normal equations have a perfect fit that is unique only by luck, and one predictor short of that they have a perfect fit and no reason at all to prefer it over the infinitely many others. R² = 1 and a training MSE of zero become automatic, which means neither is evidence of anything — and the rulers from 6.1.3 break too, because they all divide by σ̂², which has no denominator left. The section's good news is that the less flexible methods of sections 6.1 to 6.3 are exactly the right tools, since they refuse to fit as hard as they are able. Its rule about language is blunt: with p > n you may never quote a training R², an SSE or a p-value, and the only honest scorecards left are an independent test set or cross-validation.

What you will know afterwards

  • “high-dimensional” means more features than observations
  • why the worry starts before p reaches n
  • at p ≥ n a perfect fit is automatic, so it says nothing
  • why Cp, AIC and BIC cannot be computed here
  • adjusted R² can be exactly 1 too
  • the curse of dimensionality: more features, worse model
  • noise features carry cost and no upside
  • with p > n, every variable is a combination of the rest
  • the scorecards you are still allowed to use

1. What counts as high-dimensional

The book's definition is one sentence long: data sets containing more features than observations are often referred to as high-dimensional. It is worth noticing what that definition is not. It is not a statement about how many features are a lot. A model with four hundred features and ten thousand patients is a large model in a small problem. What matters is the ratio, and specifically whether p has caught up with n.

Two examples in the section make the scale concrete. Collecting half a million single-nucleotide polymorphisms for a blood-pressure study gives n ≈ 200 and p ≈ 500,000 — two hundred and fifty measurements per person. Treating every search term a user has ever typed as a feature gives a similar picture: a few hundred or a few thousand consenting users, each scored present or absent on an enormous vocabulary. In both cases the expensive thing to collect is a person, and the cheap thing to collect is another feature.

Then comes the part that makes the section apply to ordinary work. The book says the considerations it is about to raise “certainly also apply if p is slightly smaller than n, and are best always kept in mind when performing supervised learning.” The boundary p = n is where the mathematics changes character, not where the trouble starts.

Figure 6.22 is the picture that pins the idea down. Its left panel is the familiar case: one feature, an intercept, twenty observations, and a line that passes through the cloud as well as it can without touching most of the points. Its right panel is the same one feature and the same intercept with only two observations, and there the line goes exactly through both, always, whatever the data. Both panels are a valid least squares fit. The difference is that the left one has to compromise and the right one does not — and a fit that never has to compromise has learned nothing that generalises.

p ≥ n is not overfitting, it is an underdetermined system

Section 6.1 warned that a flexible model overfits because it has enough freedom to chase the noise. That is a warning about degree. At p ≥ n something sharper happens: the book's own phrasing is that least squares “cannot (or rather, should not) be performed”, because it will always produce estimates with zero residuals regardless of whether any relationship exists. The problem is no longer that a wrong answer is likely. It is that the question no longer has one right answer to be wrong about.

2. What goes wrong, and how quickly

The book's recipe for Figure 6.23 is deliberately unkind: simulate twenty observations, then run least squares with between one and twenty features, every one of which is completely unrelated to the response. There is no signal to find. The three panels report training R², training MSE, and the MSE on an independent test set.

The first two panels go where the algebra says they must. R² climbs to 1 as features are added and the training MSE falls to 0, and this happens for the same reason it happened with two observations in Figure 6.22: past a certain point the model has more free parameters than there are rows to constrain them, so it can hit every point. Neither curve is a measurement of anything.

The third panel is the measurement. Test MSE — scored on two thousand rows that were never used for fitting — climbs as features are added, because each new coefficient is fitted to a specific wobble in twenty rows and carries that wobble out into the world. The book's recommendation at the end of the passage is worth quoting exactly: we recommend always evaluating model performance on an independent test set.

Notice what the last readout says at nineteen features, and why it matters beyond this one demo. Section 6.1.3's three rulers all depend on the residual variance estimate σ̂², and that estimate is a ratio:

$$\hat{\sigma}^2 \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n - p - 1}$$

At nineteen features on twenty rows the denominator is zero, and the numerator is zero beside it. The book notes the fully high-dimensional case directly: “the formula for σ̂² from Chapter 3 yields an estimate σ̂² = 0 in this setting.” So Cp, AIC and BIC “are not appropriate in the high-dimensional setting, because estimating σ̂² is problematic”, and adjusted R² has the mirror-image failure: one can easily obtain a model with an adjusted R² of exactly 1. Every ruler from section 6.1.3 was built on the assumption that there are leftover observations to learn the noise level from, and there are none.

R² = 1 is not a result

The dangerous part is not that the model is bad. It is that the model reports itself as perfect in the same language a good model would use. R² = 1 and a training MSE of zero are what you get when you have run out of constraints, and they are available to anyone who adds enough columns — which is why section 6.4.4 ends by removing them from the list of things you are allowed to report. The test panel in the demo above is the only one of the three that is talking about the world.

3. How much of it the less flexible methods fix

Section 6.4.3's answer is short and it is the point of the whole chapter: forward stepwise selection, ridge regression, the lasso and principal components regression “are particularly useful for performing regression in the high-dimensional setting”, because they “avoid overfitting by using a less flexible fitting approach than least squares.” The chapter's methods were never designed for this case; they just happen to be the right shape for it, because each one refuses to spend every degree of freedom it is offered.

How well do they do? Figure 6.24 answers with the lasso on three simulated problems — p = 20, 50 or 2,000 features, twenty of them real, on 100 training observations — scored on an independent test set, and the book draws three conclusions out of it: regularisation plays a key role in high-dimensional problems; choosing the tuning parameter well is crucial; and test error tends to rise as dimensionality rises unless the extra features are genuinely associated with the response.

That third one is what the book names the curse of dimensionality. The natural instinct — more measurements should mean a better model — is wrong for the features that carry no information: they raise the dimensionality, they may pick up chance associations with the training response and be handed non-zero coefficients for it, and they deliver no upside in exchange. New measurement technology is described as a double-edged sword for exactly this reason.

A difference between ours and the book's worth naming. Figure 6.24 reports three fixed tuning parameters per panel and reads the answer off where the boxes are lowest; ours reports the one λ that ten-fold cross-validation actually chose, once per replicate, which is the decision a person with this data would have to make. The conclusions match — least shrinkage wins at p = 20, real shrinkage at p = 50, and nothing much helps at p = 2,000 — but they are two different experiments and we would rather say so than quietly substitute one for the other.

Adding features is a trade, not a free win

Section 6.4.3 states the rule in one line: extra features that are genuinely associated with the response reduce test error, and extra noise features raise it. Both halves matter, because they mean the direction of the effect is set by the relevance of what you measured, not by how much of it you measured. And even when the features are relevant, the variance of fitting their coefficients can outweigh the reduction in bias they bring — the same trade-off as section 2.4, played at a scale where it decides the outcome before any tuning begins.

4. What you are allowed to say about the result

The last subsection is about language, and it is the part most likely to change what you write down. When p > n, the book points out, multicollinearity stops being an inconvenience and becomes total: “any variable in the model can be written as a linear combination of all of the other variables in the model.” Which means you can never establish exactly which variables are genuinely predictive, and never identify a unique best set of coefficients. At most you can hope to put large coefficients on variables that are correlated with the ones that really matter.

The worked example makes the consequence concrete. Suppose forward stepwise selection picks 17 SNPs out of half a million for predicting blood pressure. It would be wrong to conclude those 17 SNPs predict blood pressure better than the other 499,983 — there are likely to be many sets of 17 that predict just as well, and run the same procedure on an independent sample and you would probably get a different set, possibly a non-overlapping one. That does not make the model useless; it makes the model one candidate among many, and it must be validated on independent data before anyone builds on it.

Then the rule, stated without hedging: since it is easy to obtain a useless model with zero residuals, “one should never use sum of squared errors, p-values, R² statistics, or other traditional measures of model fit on the training data as evidence of a good model fit in the high-dimensional setting.” Reporting R² = 1 in that setting is described as actively misleading — it sounds like a statistically valid model and is no evidence at all. What replaces it: an independent test set, or cross-validation errors. The demo two sections above is the shape of that replacement, and the difference between its three panels is exactly why.

Section 6.4 in one line

When there are more features than rows, least squares fits perfectly by construction, so its perfect fit, its R², its residuals and its p-values all stop carrying information, and σ̂² — the quantity the rulers of section 6.1.3 divide by — has no denominator left. The remedy is the rest of the chapter: fit less flexibly, choose the amount of shrinkage by cross-validation, and report the score on data the model has never seen. Section 6.4.4 adds the last clause: be careful what you claim about which variables, because with p > n the data cannot tell you.

Source: ISLP, Section 6.4 (pp. 263–267). Both demos simulate their own data, and both say so in the caption: the first is twenty training rows and two thousand test rows of pure noise from a stated seed, fitted by least squares live in the browser for every value of p on the slider; the second reads CH06_HD, which holds 100 replicates at each of three feature counts, each replicate a degrees-of-freedom count, a test MSE and an intercept-only test MSE, generated in tools/ch06_data.py with ten-fold cross-validation picking the tuning parameter inside the training set.
