Dimension reduction methods

Sections 6.1 and 6.2 argued about which of the original predictors to trust. Section 6.3 stops arguing and changes the predictors: build M new variables, each a weighted sum of the originals, then run least squares on those M as if they were the data. The weights are the whole question. Principal components choose them by following the spread of the data and never look at the response. Partial least squares looks at the response first and weights each predictor by how well it predicts. The three demos below rotate a line through our own 100 simulated cities, rebuild the Credit model out of its first M components with cross-validation picking M, and put the book's Figure 6.21 on a slider.

Reading

  • Read Section 6.3 in full (pp. 254–262). The opening page states the recipe shared by every method in the section; 6.3.1 does principal components at length, 6.3.2 does partial least squares in about a page.
  • Skim Equations (6.16) to (6.18). It is the shortest passage in the chapter and it says one thing: replace the p predictors by M combinations, fit least squares to those, and notice that this forces the original coefficients into the shape of (6.18) — a constraint, and therefore a trade.
  • Then Section 6.4, which asks what happens when p grows past n and the machinery of 6.1 to 6.3 stops being a choice and starts being the only thing between you and a model that fits noise perfectly.

Key ideas

Every method so far used the original predictors; this one replaces them with M linear combinations of them, which constrains the original coefficients to the form of (6.18) and trades a little bias for a lot less variance when M is well below p. The only question is how to choose the weights, and there are two answers. Principal components pick the direction the data spreads along furthest — an unsupervised answer that knows nothing about the response, which is exactly why the number of components has to be chosen by cross-validation, and why a component carrying 2% of the predictors' variance can be the one that fixes the model. Partial least squares weights each predictor by its simple regression on the response. On our simulated cities that tilts the line away from ad spending, because population is both the more variable column and the better predictor of sales — and pushing advertising's weight up swings PLS past the PC line and keeps going, which is the entire difference between a direction chosen by the spread of X and one chosen by X's relationship to y.

What you will know afterwards

  • M new variables as weighted sums of the p old ones

  • the first PC: widest spread and closest line, one line
  • reading PC loadings as a direction in the raw units
  • why 97.6% of the variance can still be a useless model
  • M is a tuning parameter, chosen by cross-validation
  • PLS: weight by the response, then deflate

1. Fit the transformed variables

Here is the move in one sentence: instead of estimating p + 1 coefficients on the original predictors, first mash the predictors down into M of them — M smaller than p — and estimate M + 1 coefficients on those. Each new variable is a weighted sum of the originals, with weights φjm that some rule has to supply:

$$Z_m \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_{jm}\, X_j \qquad m = 1, \dots, M \qquad(6.16)$$

Once you have them, the second step is ordinary least squares with the Z's standing in for the X's:

$$y_i \;=\; \theta_0 + \sum_{m=1}^{M} \theta_m z_{im} + \varepsilon_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n \qquad(6.17)$$

Two steps, always the same two: build the new variables, then fit. The two approaches in this section differ only in how they do the first step. But there is a consequence worth sitting with, because it is the reason the section exists at all. Substitute (6.16) into (6.17) and the model collapses back onto the original predictors with coefficients that are forced into a particular shape:

$$\beta_j \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{M} \theta_m \phi_{jm} \qquad(6.18)$$

Those βj are no longer free to be whatever the data wants. They must be a combination of M fixed directions, and if M is much smaller than p that is a real restriction — one that can bias the estimates. It earns its keep through variance: when p is large relative to n, forcing the coefficients onto M well-chosen directions is often the difference between a model that predicts and a model that memorises. And if M = p with the Z's independent, (6.18) constrains nothing and you are simply running least squares again. The second demo on this page checks exactly that: set M to 11 and its M readout prints the largest difference between the two coefficient vectors — a rounding error next to coefficients that run into the hundreds.

The one question this section asks

Nothing above says which combinations to use. That single choice splits the section in two. Principal components answer by looking only at X — find the directions along which the predictors vary most, in order, and keep the first M. Partial least squares answers by looking at both — weight each predictor by how strongly it is related to the response. The first is unsupervised and therefore indifferent to whether the spread it captures has anything to do with y; the second is supervised and therefore can chase noise. Everything else in section 6.3 follows from that fork.

2. Principal components: aim the line at the spread

The first principal component direction is defined by a claim about pictures: it is the line along which the observations vary the most. Project every point onto the line — drop each one onto the nearest position on it — and those projected positions will be more spread out than they would be on any other line you could draw.

There is a second way to say the same thing, and the book gives both because they are genuinely two views of one object. The line that maximises the spread of the projections is also the line that minimises the sum of the squared perpendicular distances from the points to the line. Fix the total: every point's distance from the cloud's centre splits between along-the-line and off-the-line, so pushing the projections apart necessarily pulls the perpendicular residuals in. Demo 1 hands you the line and reports both numbers at once, so you can watch one go down while the other goes up.

Written out, the first component is a single weighted average of the two columns, with the weights fixed so that they cannot be inflated — otherwise you could make the variance as large as you liked by simply scaling up:

$$Z_1 \;=\; \phi_{11}\big(\mathrm{pop} - \overline{\mathrm{pop}}\big) \;+\; \phi_{21}\big(\mathrm{ad} - \overline{\mathrm{ad}}\big), \qquad \phi_{11}^2 + \phi_{21}^2 = 1 \qquad(6.19)$$

Those φ's are called the loadings, and they are read as a direction: (6.19) is one line in the pop–ad plane, and the line with the largest possible projection variance under the length constraint is the one the picture draws. Per observation the same combination gives a single number, the score:

$$z_{i1} \;=\; \phi_{11}\big(\mathrm{pop}_i - \overline{\mathrm{pop}}\big) \;+\; \phi_{21}\big(\mathrm{ad}_i - \overline{\mathrm{ad}}\big) \qquad(6.20)$$

So one column replaces two: a city's population and its ad budget collapse into a single "how big is this place" number, which the book argues works well here precisely because the two columns are close to linearly related. On the book's own cities the loadings come out at 0.839 and 0.544 — both positive and of similar size, so Z1 is, as the book puts it, almost an average of the two. The demo below computes ours.

A note on conventions, because the difference is visible. We draw (6.19) in the original units: a unit-length combination of the two columns themselves — 0.839² + 0.544² is 1 — which is what makes the dashed drops in the left panel genuinely perpendicular and "closest line" mean closest in these units. The book's footnote 7 says its own figure was computed after standardising pop and ad first, which points the line at slope 0.74 instead of our 0.58; we follow the equation as printed, on our data, and say so rather than quietly pick one.

Two things the demo makes easy to check. First, the loadings are both positive and of similar size, so the score is close to an average of the two budgets — the book's reasoning for expecting a single number to work well. Second, the split of the variance: PC1 takes 80.1% and PC2 19.9%, and PC2 is by construction uncorrelated with PC1, so it represents what the first component missed. In this cloud there is not much left to miss, which is what the book's "pop and ad have approximately a linear relationship" looks like in numbers.

3. Principal components regression, and how to choose M

Now the regression. Take the first M principal components, fit (6.17) to them, and map the answer back through (6.18) to get coefficients on the original predictors. The book is explicit about one preliminary: standardise every predictor first, using (6.6), because otherwise the high-variance columns dominate the components and the units you happened to measure in silently decide the answer. Every predictor measured in the same units is the exception it allows.

That leaves M, and M cannot be read off the variance shares. This is the trap the section is built around, and the Credit data walks straight into it: the first nine components carry 97.6% of the predictors' variance and produce a model that is nowhere near least squares; the tenth component carries the last 2.4% and the model falls off a cliff. Cross-validation, not the scree plot, has to pick M — and the demo computes the whole curve live, refitting the decomposition inside every one of the ten folds.

Variance explained is not relevance

Principal components are unsupervised: they rank directions by how much of X's spread they capture and are simply not told that the response exists. Usually the big directions also happen to matter. Credit is a clean counterexample, and it is worth remembering because the fix is not a better scree plot — it is a supervised criterion, either cross-validation over M as above, or a method that consults y while choosing the directions in the first place. That second option is the whole of section 6.3.2.

4. Partial least squares: let the response vote

Partial least squares keeps the two-step shape of (6.16) and (6.17) and changes only how the first direction is picked. Standardise the predictors, then set each weight φj1 to the coefficient from the simple linear regression of Y on Xj. The book points out the shortcut that makes this cheap to think about: that coefficient is proportional to the correlation between Y and Xj. So the first PLS direction is, up to a normalisation, just the vector of correlations with the response — heavy weight on whichever predictors actually move the needle.

$$\phi_{j1} \;\propto\; \mathrm{cor}(Y,\, X_j) \qquad\text{after standardising the predictors}$$

Everything after that mirrors PCR: deflate each variable and y by their fit on the new score, repeat for as many directions as you want, then run least squares on the resulting Z's. The book is candid about the trade — supervised directions can reduce bias, but they can also increase variance, and in practice PLS "often performs no better than ridge regression or PCR". It is a different bias–variance wager, not a free upgrade.

Section 6.3 in one line

Dimension reduction replaces the p predictors with M linear combinations of them and fits least squares on the combinations, which constrains the original coefficients to (6.18) and buys lower variance at the price of some bias. Principal components choose the combinations from the spread of X alone — a reason the number of components must be picked by cross-validation rather than by how much variance the components explain — while partial least squares chooses them from each predictor's relationship to y, which can help and can just as easily add variance. Both are attempts to answer the same question: which directions in the predictor cloud are worth keeping.
