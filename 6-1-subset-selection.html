Subset selection

You have a pile of candidate predictors and one response. Which of them belong in the model? Section 6.1 answers in two moves: search a set of candidate models — every subset, or a path walked one variable at a time — then pick the model's size with a ruler that estimates test error, because the training error alone can only ever vote for the biggest model. The three demos below run the whole search and all the rulers live on the book's 400-row Credit data, so every frontier and every minimum is one your browser just computed.

Reading

  • Read Section 6.1 in full (pp. 230–240). It opens the chapter: three subsections, one for the search (best subset and the two stepwise walks) and one for the rulers that choose the size.
  • Skim 6.1.2 only for the bookkeeping. The two stepwise algorithms are short, and the ideas they encode — start empty, add what helps most, stop when nothing does — are already in 6.1.1 and 6.1.3. Forward stepwise is the one worth remembering; backward needs n > p.
  • Then Section 6.2, which changes the question. Instead of choosing a subset and fitting it exactly, shrinkage keeps every predictor but pulls the coefficients toward zero — a smoother, often better-behaved way out of the same overfitting trap.

Key ideas

Adding a variable can never make the training RSS worse, so RSS and R² always crown the full model and are useless for choosing a size. You must either adjust the training error for the number of variables — the rulers Cp, AIC, BIC and adjusted R², each a price per variable added to the RSS — or estimate the test error directly with the validation set or cross-validation from Chapter 5. The search itself comes in three flavours: fit every subset (best subset), or walk forward from empty / backward from full, one variable at a time. On the Credit data the book's BIC stops at four variables and its Cp at six — and the demos below name the exact same ones.

1. Why training error cannot choose

Suppose you have ten candidate predictors and you want to know which subset belongs in the model. The naive answer is "fit them all and keep whichever combination gives the lowest training error." That answer is broken, and the book says so in one line: the model containing all the predictors will always have the smallest RSS and the largest R², because those quantities are tied to the training error and adding a variable can only ever pull the fitted line closer to the data it is being fitted to.

So RSS and R² are disqualified as rulers. To choose a size you need an estimate of test error, and there are exactly two ways to get one, which the book lays out before touching any method:

  • Adjust the training error for the fact that it under-counts. Adding variables always lowers RSS but not necessarily test error, so add back a penalty that grows with the number of variables. Four rulers do this: Cp, AIC, BIC and adjusted R².
  • Estimate the test error directly, by holding data back — the validation-set and cross-validation machinery of Chapter 5, reused here with one twist: the model's size is now the thing being chosen.

The one idea behind every ruler

Every criterion in this section is a balance of two forces. One term rewards the model for fitting the data (it falls as variables are added). The other charges a fixed price for each variable included (it rises as variables are added). The best size is where their sum bottoms out. Cp and AIC charge the same price, BIC charges more, adjusted R² folds the charge into the denominator — and the validation and CV rulers skip the algebra and measure the test error by holding data back.

2. Best subset selection: fit every model

The most direct search is to fit all 2p subsets of the predictors and keep the best of each size. With ten predictors that is 210 = 1,024 models — a thousand, entirely affordable. With twenty it is over a million, and the book is blunt that beyond roughly forty predictors the search becomes infeasible. The counting is the first thing to internalise, and it is why the stepwise methods in the next subsection exist.

Here is the whole search on the Credit data, run the moment the page loaded. One wrinkle the book makes explicit: one of the ten predictors, Ethnicity, is categorical with three levels, so it enters as two dummy variables — the book says these are "selected separately in this case". That is why the design has eleven columns and the models range over sizes 1 to 11, exactly as Figure 6.1 does. Each of the 2,047 non-empty subsets is a dot; the red line is the frontier, the best model at each size, and it is the frontier that every ruler in section 4 will read.

The frontier is the search's real output

Best subset selection does not return one model — it returns the lower envelope of every model you could fit, one winner per size. Everything after this is a question about where on the red line to stop, and that question is what section 4's rulers answer. Nothing about the frontier itself tells you the right size; a lower RSS at size eleven is guaranteed and therefore says nothing.

3. Walking instead of jumping

Best subset is exhaustive but its cost doubles with every added predictor. The book's fix is to trade exhaustiveness for a short walk. Forward stepwise starts from the empty model and, at each step, adds the single variable that most reduces RSS — then repeats until nothing helps. Backward stepwise does the reverse, starting from the full model and dropping the least useful variable at each step. (Backward needs n > p so the full model can be fit at all; forward does not, which makes it the only option when the predictors outnumber the observations — a point that matters again in section 6.4.)

The cost is the payoff. A forward walk over p predictors evaluates p + (p−1) + ⋯ + 1 variables at its steps, so about 1 + p(p+1)/2 models in total — for the book's p = 20 example, 211 models instead of 1,048,576. Forward stepwise "tends to do well in practice", the book says, but it is not guaranteed to find the best subset — a greedy walk can commit to a first variable that locks it out of a better pair further along. In practice that rarely bites, and the computational saving is enormous. That is the trade, and it is usually worth it.

4. Choosing the size

Now the rulers. Each one takes the frontier — the best RSS at every size — and adds a penalty per variable, then reports the size where the total is smallest. All four are estimated on the full model's error variance σ̂², which for Credit comes out at about 9,760.

Cp and AIC are the same formula for least squares, and they add a penalty of 2dσ̂² to the training RSS, scaled down by n. Note the parentheses: the 1/n divides the RSS and the penalty.

$$C_p \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathrm{RSS} \;+\; 2\,d\,\hat{\sigma}^2\Big) \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n} + \frac{2\,d\,\hat{\sigma}^2}{n} \qquad(6.2)$$

BIC swaps that penalty for log(n)·dσ̂², and since log n exceeds 2 for any n bigger than seven, BIC charges more per variable and so stops at a smaller model.

$$\mathrm{BIC} \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathrm{RSS} \;+\; \log(n)\,d\,\hat{\sigma}^2\Big) \qquad(6.3)$$

Adjusted R² takes a different route: it divides RSS by the degrees of freedom left over, so that padding a correct model with noise variables starts to hurt the score — unlike plain R², which only ever improves. A large adjusted R² means a small test error, so this ruler is maximised rather than minimised.

$$\mathrm{Adjusted}\;R^2 \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-d-1)}{\mathrm{TSS}/(n-1)} \qquad(6.4)$$

The adjusted-training-error rulers are convenient but indirect. The direct route reuses Chapter 5: split off a validation set, or run ten-fold CV, and for each model size fit the best subset on the training part then score it on the held-back part. The book's Figure 6.3 does both, on a three-quarter/one-quarter split and ten folds, and puts them beside √BIC so all three share one scale. Its conclusion: validation and cross-validation both land on a six-variable model, but the curves are so flat that the four-, five- and six-variable models are roughly equivalent — and the precise winner would change if you reshuffled the split or the folds. The demo lets you do exactly that reshuffling.

Section 6.1 in one line

To pick which predictors belong, first search a set of candidate models — best subset if you can afford it, forward stepwise if you cannot — then choose the size with a ruler that estimates test error rather than training error: adjust the RSS with Cp, AIC, BIC or adjusted R², or estimate the test error directly with a validation set or cross-validation. Training RSS and R² can never make the choice, because they always prefer the largest model regardless of whether it predicts.
