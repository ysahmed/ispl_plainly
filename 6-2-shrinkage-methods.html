Shrinkage methods

Section 6.1 threw predictors away to control variance. Section 6.2 keeps all of them and does something subtler: keep every predictor, but refuse to trust any coefficient fully — add a penalty that rewards coefficients for being small, and let one number, λ, decide how much to trust the data. Two penalties are on offer. Squared coefficients give ridge, which shrinks everything by the same proportion and never deletes anything. Absolute coefficients give the lasso, which subtracts the same amount from everything and deletes the small ones outright. The five demos below draw both paths, the geometry that explains them, the operator behind soft-thresholding, and two cross-validation curves computed live.

Reading

  • Read Section 6.2 in full (pp. 241–253). Three subsections: ridge, the lasso, and choosing λ. The geometry and the Bayesian aside are the parts that make the rest of the chapter make sense.
  • Skim 6.2.2's Bayesian interpretation (the Gaussian and Laplace priors, Figure 6.11). It is the shortest path to an intuition for why one penalty deletes and the other does not — but you can skip it and still follow everything else.
  • Then Section 6.3, which gives up on the original predictors altogether and builds a smaller set of new ones from their combinations — the third way to fight the same variance problem.

Key ideas

Ridge and the lasso fit all p predictors at once, but solve min RSS + λ × (a penalty on the coefficients) instead of plain least squares. Ridge's penalty is λ Σβj² — an ℓ2 penalty — which shrinks every coefficient toward zero by the same proportion and never sets any exactly to zero. The lasso's penalty is λ Σ|βj| — an ℓ1 penalty — which subtracts the same amount from every coefficient, wiping out any that fall inside the dead zone. That one difference is the whole story: ridge gives you a dense, shrunk model; the lasso gives you a sparse one and does variable selection as a side effect. λ is chosen by cross-validation, exactly like the model size was in section 6.1.

1. Why shrink at all

Least squares has no opinions. It picks whatever coefficients fit the training data best, and if a predictor happens to look useful by luck, least squares will happily give it a large coefficient. With many predictors — or correlated ones — that luck-driven variance is exactly what ruins predictions on new data.

Shrinkage is the fix. The book puts it plainly: instead of choosing a subset and fitting it exactly, we "fit a model containing all p predictors using a technique that constrains or regularizes the coefficient estimates", and it states the payoff in one sentence — "shrinking the coefficient estimates can significantly reduce their variance." The cost is bias: a shrunken coefficient is systematically too close to zero. The trade is only worth it when the variance you buy back exceeds the bias you pay, which is why λ has to be chosen rather than guessed.

Two ways to say the same thing

The book writes shrinkage twice on purpose: as a penalty added to the RSS, and as a constraint imposed on the coefficients. "For every value of λ, there is some s such that" the two forms "give the same" estimates. The penalty form is what you optimise; the constraint form is what you can see — which is why the geometry demo in section 3 is drawn as a budget you cannot exceed.

2. Ridge and the lasso

Ridge regression adds a penalty of λ times the sum of squared coefficients. Small coefficients are cheap; large ones are expensive, quadratically so.

$$\text{ridge} \;=\; \mathrm{RSS} \;+\; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2 \qquad(6.5)$$

The book warns that the answer depends on the scale of each predictor, so ridge and lasso are always run on standardised columns — centred and divided by their standard deviation — before the penalty is applied. That is equation (6.6), and it is why every coefficient picture in this section is on a common, unitless scale.

$$\tilde{x}_{ij} \;=\; \frac{x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j}{s_j} \qquad(6.6)$$

The lasso is identical except that squares become absolute values:

$$\text{lasso} \;=\; \mathrm{RSS} \;+\; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \qquad(6.7)$$

"Comparing (6.7) to (6.5), we see that the lasso and ridge regression have similar formulations. The only difference is that the βj² term … has been replaced by |βj|." The book calls these the ℓ2 and ℓ1 penalties, and the consequences of that one-character difference are enormous: ridge "will shrink all of the coefficients towards zero, but it will not set any of them exactly to zero (unless λ = ∞)", while the lasso "has the effect of forcing some of the coefficient estimates to be exactly equal to zero when the tuning parameter λ is sufficiently large."

The one-character difference

Both penalties are smallest at the origin, so both pull coefficients toward zero. But a square grows faster the further you move, so the ℓ2 penalty punishes a single large coefficient far more heavily than several moderate ones — ridge would rather spread the fit around than let anything get big. The ℓ1 penalty grows linearly, so near zero it is steep enough that a coefficient sitting a hair below the threshold gains nothing by being nudged across it. That flat spot at the origin is why the lasso can leave a coefficient exactly at zero, and ridge cannot.

3. What the two penalties actually do

The cleanest way to see the difference is to stop thinking of the penalty as a term in an equation and start thinking of it as a budget. Minimising RSS subject to a limit on the coefficients is the same problem:

$$\min \ \mathrm{RSS} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \le s \qquad(6.8)$$ $$\min \ \mathrm{RSS} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^{2} \le s \qquad(6.9)$$

Now the answer is a picture: the RSS is a set of ellipses centred on the least-squares estimate, and the constraint is a shape you are not allowed to leave. The best feasible coefficients are where the smallest ellipse you can reach just touches that shape. Figure 6.7 draws exactly this, and the two shapes are the whole difference — a diamond for the lasso, a circle for ridge.

That is the geometric reason, but it is worth having the arithmetic version too, because it is the one that shows up in every lasso implementation. For the idealised case where the predictors are uncorrelated and standardised, both penalties collapse to closed forms. Ridge gives

$$\hat{\beta}_j^{R} \;=\; \frac{y_j}{1 + \lambda} \qquad(6.14)$$

— every coefficient divided by the same factor, so the estimate is always a fixed fraction of the least-squares one. The lasso gives

$$\hat{\beta}_j^{L} \;=\; \begin{cases} y_j - \lambda/2 & y_j > \lambda/2\\ y_j + \lambda/2 & y_j < -\lambda/2\\ 0 & |y_j| \le \lambda/2 \end{cases} \qquad(6.15)$$

— every coefficient reduced by the same amount, λ/2, and set exactly to zero if that subtraction would cross the origin. The book names this operation: "the least squares coefficients that are less than λ/2 in absolute value are shrunken entirely to zero … known as soft-thresholding. The fact that some lasso coefficients are shrunken entirely to zero explains why the lasso performs feature selection."

Why this is not just a technicality

Proportional shrinkage means ridge's coefficients are all still fighting each other for credit — with correlated predictors like Limit and Rating, ridge splits the credit between them in a ratio that depends on their correlation, and neither coefficient means much on its own. Soft-thresholding means the lasso resolves that argument by switching most of the participants off. It is a coarser, blunter tool, and that bluntness is precisely what makes the result readable.

4. Choosing λ

Every claim above is conditional on λ. λ = 0 is plain least squares; λ → ∞ is the empty model. The book is explicit that "selecting a good value of λ for the lasso is critical", and the method is the one you already know: "Cross-validation provides a simple way to tackle this problem. We choose a grid of λ values, and compute the cross-validation error for each value of λ … We then select the tuning parameter value for which the cross-validation error is smallest."

The first example is ridge on Credit, using leave-one-out cross-validation. Doing that naively means 400 refits per λ, but a ridge fit is a linear smoother, so one eigendecomposition gives every leave-one-out residual in a single pass — the whole path of 60 λ values is computed while the page loads.

The second example is the lasso on data where sparsity is the right answer. The book's Figure 6.9/6.13 simulation has 45 predictors but only two that matter — "the response is a function of only 2 out of 45 predictors" — and the question is whether cross-validation can find them. The book's answer: "Not only has the lasso correctly given much larger coefficient estimates to the two signal predictors, but also the minimum cross-validation error corresponds to a set of coefficient estimates for which only" the signal variables are non-zero.

Our version of that experiment is described in full in the caption, because the book does not print its simulation parameters: n = 50, p = 45 standard-normal predictors, two of them carrying coefficients of +4 and −4, unit Gaussian noise, with the data and fold seeds fixed so the picture is reproducible.

So which penalty should you use? The book refuses to pick a universal winner, and the reason is the bias-variance trade-off wearing different clothes: "one might expect the lasso to perform better in a setting where a relatively small number of predictors have substantial coefficients, and the remaining predictors have coefficients that are very small or that equal zero. Ridge regression will perform better when the response is a function of many predictors, all with coefficients of roughly equal size." The catch, immediately after: "the number of predictors that is related to the response is never known a priori for real data sets." Cross-validation is how you find out, and that is what the demos above are for.

The Bayesian shortcut, in one paragraph

Section 6.2.2 offers a different way to remember the difference. Assume normal errors and a prior on the coefficients that factorises one coefficient at a time. If that prior is Gaussian, the posterior mode — and the posterior mean — is the ridge solution. If it is a double-exponential (Laplace) distribution, the posterior mode is the lasso solution. The book's summary is the bit worth keeping: "the lasso prior is steeply peaked at zero, while the Gaussian is flatter and fatter at zero. Hence, the lasso expects a priori that many of the coefficients are (exactly) zero, while ridge assumes the coefficients are randomly distributed about zero."

Section 6.2 in one line

Keep every predictor but shrink the coefficients: ridge's squared penalty reduces them all by the same proportion and never deletes one, while the lasso's absolute-value penalty reduces them by the same amount and deletes outright whatever falls inside the dead zone — which is why only the lasso does variable selection. Choose λ the way you chose model size in section 6.1, with cross-validation, and remember that the CV curve is often so flat that a wide range of λ would serve equally well.
