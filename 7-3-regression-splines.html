Regression splines

Section 7.1 made one smooth curve bend all the way across the age range; section 7.2 threw the curve away and fit flat pieces with jumps between them. Splines are the settlement. Cut the predictor into pieces, fit a polynomial in each piece — and then glue the pieces so that the joins do not show. The whole section is about how many pieces, where to cut, how smooth the glue has to be, and what happens at the two ends where there is nothing left to glue to.

Reading

  • Read Section 7.4 in full (pp. 295–300). Five short subsections, equations (7.8) to (7.10), and four figures — the densest stretch of the chapter, and the one you will actually use.
  • Skim Equation (7.8) and the truncated power function (7.10). These say one useful thing: a spline fit is an ordinary least squares problem. Once you believe that, every standard error, F test and confidence band from chapter 3 comes along for free.
  • Then Section 7.5, which replaces the knots with a smoothness parameter and lets the data decide how wiggly the curve should be. The two are cousins: a smoothing spline and a regression spline with enough knots reach almost the same place.

Key ideas

A regression spline splits the range of X at K knots and fits a separate polynomial in each piece, with constraints forcing the pieces to join. Degree d with K knots and d − 1 continuous derivatives is the standard choice; in practice that means cubic, always. Every constraint imposed removes one degree of freedom, which is why the book counts coefficients the way it does: a cubic spline with K knots costs $K+4$ of them, and a natural cubic spline — which also demands straight lines outside the outer knots — costs $K+1$. The truncated power basis of equation (7.10) turns all of this into a linear model, so it is fitted by the same least squares as everything before it. And because the knots are arbitrary, the number of them is a tuning parameter, chosen the way chapter 5 chose everything else: by cross-validation, on a curve that turns out to be very flat.

1. A polynomial that is allowed to break

Start with the unconstrained idea. Pick a knot — age 50, say — and fit one cubic below it and an unrelated cubic above it. The book's words for the result are worth quoting exactly: the function "is discontinuous and looks ridiculous!". Two polynomials of degree three means eight coefficients, and the book counts those as eight degrees of freedom. There is nothing gluing the two halves together, so at the knot the fitted curve simply steps sideways.

Now impose continuity: require the two cubics to take the same value at age 50. That is one linear constraint on eight coefficients, so the model drops to seven. The book's comment on the result is that "the V-shaped join looks unnatural" — the curve no longer jumps, but its slope does. Impose two more constraints, that the first and second derivatives agree at the knot, and you have the cubic spline of the next section: three constraints gone, five coefficients left.

Each constraint is a promise about the fitted function, and promises have prices. The demo prints all four prices at once, as residual sums of squares.

At the default knot of 50 the four residual sums of squares are 4,759,774 for the unconstrained fit, 4,760,938 for the continuous one, 4,768,952 for the cubic spline and 4,884,863 for the linear spline. Read the gaps rather than the numbers. Forcing continuity — the one constraint that removes the ridiculous jump — costs 1,164 out of 4.76 million. Adding the two derivative constraints costs 8,014 more. Requiring the curve to be made of straight segments instead of cubics costs 115,911: a hundred times the continuity constraint, and thirteen times everything the gluing bought. Smoothness is nearly free; being cubic is what you pay for.

Drag the knot and the same ranking holds at every setting the slider offers between 40 and 60, though the individual numbers move: the unconstrained RSS runs between 4,757,865 with the knot at 52.5 and 4,766,882 with it at 40.5 — nine thousand out of 4.76 million, which is what "the data has no opinion about where you put the knot" looks like in numbers. The size of the jump changes sign on the way, running from −5.24 at 40 through zero to +3.47 at 60. That sign flip is a small reminder that the jump is a property of the fit, not of the data: nothing in the salary record says a man's wage should step down or step up on his fiftieth birthday, and the unconstrained model is happy to say either.

Why not fit every piece separately?

You could, and it would be worse. Fitting two independent cubics on either side of 50 is exactly what the top-left panel does, and it wastes data: the fit below the knot never gets to use anything above it. The constraints are not decoration. They are how the information on both sides of a knot gets pooled into one estimate at that knot — and, as the readouts show, they cost almost nothing.

2. Gluing the pieces: cubic and natural cubic

Rewriting the continuity constraints out of the model rather than into it gives the form the book actually fits. A cubic spline with K knots at $\xi_1, \ldots, \xi_K$ is written with the truncated power basis:

$$ \begin{aligned} f(x) \;&=\; \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \beta_3 x^3 \\ &\quad+ \beta_4 h(x,\xi_1) + \beta_5 h(x,\xi_2) + \cdots + \beta_{3+K} h(x,\xi_K) \end{aligned} \qquad(7.8)$$ $$h(x,\xi) \;=\; (x-\xi)_+^3 \;=\; \begin{cases} (x-\xi)^3 & x > \xi \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \qquad(7.10)$$

The book's remark about (7.10) is the whole trick: adding $\beta_4 h(x,\xi)$ to a cubic "will lead to a discontinuity in only the third derivative at $\xi$; the function will remain continuous, with continuous first and second derivatives, at each of the knots". The constraints are baked into the shape of the columns, so the fit is an ordinary least squares problem with $3+K$ predictors and $K+4$ coefficients — the book's degrees of freedom count.

That leaves a problem the book raises before it solves it: "splines can have high variance at the outer range of the predictors". Past the last knot a cubic spline is just a cubic, free to swing wherever its three leading coefficients want, and with no data beyond to pull it back. The fix is the natural cubic spline: two extra constraints, one forcing the fit to be linear below the smallest knot and one above the largest. Three constraints in total, so $K+1$ degrees of freedom instead of $K+4$, and the price is that the curve outside the data's reach is a straight line rather than a curve.

At the default three knots the cubic spline leaves an RSS of 4,766,244 and the natural cubic spline 4,773,109 — the natural fit is a little worse, as it must be, since it lives in a smaller space (5 coefficients against 7). The interesting number is the other one. At age 80 the cubic spline's 2× standard error band is 29.5 thousand dollars wide and the natural spline's is 16.8. Push the knot count down to one and the gap widens in relative terms: 24.3 against 10.4, with the natural spline's band less than half the cubic's. That is the argument for the natural constraints, in the units the book uses to make it.

One counting note, because this section uses two conventions for the same quantity and it is worth seeing them side by side. In section 7.4.1 the book counts a cubic spline's degrees of freedom as its number of coefficients, $K+4$, which is why Figure 7.3's panels are labelled 8, 7, 5 and 3. In section 7.4.4 it asks for "four degrees of freedom" to get three interior knots, which is R's convention for ns(): the number of basis columns after the intercept, $K+1$, or five coefficients in total. Both are defensible and neither is wrong; the readouts above print knots and coefficients, which nobody can misread. At three interior knots the cubic spline has seven coefficients and the natural cubic spline five.

Two checks you can make for free against the previous page. Drag the knot slider to 1, and the cubic spline here is the same model as page 7.2's truncated-power family at df 4 — one interior knot — and both report an RSS of 4,773,100. Drag it to 3, and the natural spline here is page 7.2's natural-spline family at df 4: 33.75, 42 and 51 again, and an RSS of 4,773,109 in both places. Nothing has been refitted differently. The knots are placed by the same rule, the spaces are identical, and least squares has no opinion about which page you read it on.

One space, two sets of coordinates

Page 7.2 fitted every family twice — once in the plain columns of equation (7.8), once in orthonormal columns — and showed the two fitted curves agreeing to machine precision. The same thing is happening behind every panel on this page. These demos orthonormalise their basis columns before fitting, because $x^3$ at $x=80$ is 512,000: a one-knot cubic spline written in raw powers has a design matrix with a condition number of about 8 million, and the degree-15 polynomial of section 4 written that way would have one near $10^{34}$, which is arithmetic with no significant digits left in it. The columns you would see are rotated versions of the book's, so their coefficients are not the book's coefficients; the fitted curves, residuals and standard errors are.

3. How many knots, and where

Every constraint so far was free to choose, and the book is explicit that the knots are too. Its own recommendation is unexciting and correct: "in practice it is common to place knots in a uniform fashion. One way to do this is to specify the desired degrees of freedom, and then have the software automatically place the corresponding number of knots at uniform quantiles of the data." That is what ns(age, df=4) does, and it is why the book's Figure 7.5 — four degrees of freedom — puts its three knots at exactly the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles of age. The phrase "four degrees of freedom leads to three interior knots" is the natural spline's count, $K+1$, in one line.

That leaves the number itself. The book's answer is cross-validation, and its reading of its own figure is that "three degrees of freedom for the natural spline and four degrees of freedom for the cubic spline are quite adequate". The demo recomputes that figure from scratch, ten folds at a time.

Our minima come out at 8 degrees of freedom for the natural spline (1,591.5) and 10 for the cubic (also 1,591.5) — not 3 and 4. Now look at what those values are measured against. From df 3 up to df 10 the natural spline's curve never leaves the band between 1,591.5 and 1,593.8, a range of 2.3 on a scale of 1,592. The cubic's range from df 3 to 10 is 3.0. Meanwhile the standard error printed beside every single point is about 67. Every point above df 2 is the same point drawn ten times, and which of them happens to land lowest is decided by the fold shuffle — press the button and watch the argmin walk around while the curve underneath it does not move at all. That is why the book says three and four are "quite adequate": not because they are optimal, but because nothing above them is measurably better.

Two details on the same figure are worth checking rather than trusting. The leftmost point of the natural-spline panel is df 1, which is a plain linear regression, and it comes out at 1,675.5 — the same number page 7.1 printed for its degree-1 polynomial, because a line is a line. That drop from 1,675.5 to 1,593.5 is the book's "clear evidence that a one-degree fit is not adequate", and at 82 units it is thirty-six times the entire variation of the curve above df 3. The other detail is the shape of the drop: by df 2 the natural spline is already at 1,600.7, and everything after that is refinement measured in single units. The flexibility a spline needs is bought with knots at the quantiles, and three of them buy almost all of it.

4. Splines against polynomials at the edges

Section 7.4.5 asks the question the whole chapter has been circling: if a polynomial and a spline are both basis models fitted by least squares, why prefer the spline? The book's answer is mechanical — a polynomial "must use a high degree … to produce flexible fits", while splines "introduce flexibility by increasing the number of knots but keeping the degree fixed", and that "generally, this approach produces more stable estimates". Figure 7.7 is the demonstration, and it is the most violent figure in the chapter.

At the default settings the two curves disagree by up to 61.0 thousand dollars, and the disagreement is at age 79 — one year before the end of the data. Run the list of polynomial values from age 70 to 80 and the shape of the trouble is plain: 99.5, 98.7, 97.5, 95.4, 93.2, 92.6, 97.4, 110.9, 131.8, 142.3, 86.1. It drifts down to 92.6 at age 75, climbs 49.7 over the next four years to 142.3, and then drops 56.2 in the last one. Over the same eleven ages the natural spline goes 105.0 down to 78.5, monotone, following the data. Nothing in the salary record is doing that; it is $x^{15}$ doing what $x^{15}$ always does at the ends of its range — and the peak lands in a hole. This data set contains no 78-year-olds and no 79-year-olds at all: the nearest men are one 77-year-old and four 80-year-olds, so the polynomial's 56-point dive happens over two years that hold not one observation to argue with it.

The book's own summary is that "the extra flexibility in the polynomial produces undesirable results at the boundaries, while the natural cubic spline still provides a reasonable fit to the data", and the deeper reason it gives is the one to keep: splines buy flexibility by adding knots in the region where you want the curve to move, keeping the degree fixed at three, so the model's behavior far from the data is a straight line instead of a fifteenth-power tantrum. It also notes the flexibility this buys you in design — more knots where $f$ changes fast, fewer where it is stable — before admitting that in practice everyone puts them at the quantiles anyway, which is what this page's demos do.

Section 7.4 in one line

A regression spline is a piecewise polynomial with the joints sanded smooth: constraints buy continuity almost for free, the natural constraints buy stable uncertainty at the edges for a little more, and the number of knots is a tuning parameter chosen by cross-validation on a curve so flat that almost anything above two degrees of freedom will do. When a spline and a polynomial have to guess past the end of the data, bet on the spline.

Source: ISLP, Section 7.4 (pp. 295–300), Figures 7.3, 7.4, 7.6 and 7.7. Every curve, band, residual sum of squares, cross-validated mean squared error and standard error on this page is fitted in your browser from the book's own Wage data set — 3,000 observations, ages 18 to 80, 79 of them above $250,000 — as shipped with the book's Python package. Nothing is simulated; the only random number drawn anywhere on this page is the ten-fold shuffle in the third demo, whose seed is 7 and whose button redraws it. Three conventions are ours and are stated where they are used: Figures 7.3, 7.4 and 7.7 are fitted on all 3,000 rows although the book says it plots "a subset" it does not identify, so our curves are our own and not reproductions; the knots are placed at the quantiles of age, the lab's rule, rather than by any other scheme; and the basis columns are orthonormalised before fitting because the raw power basis is badly conditioned, so the coefficients are rotated versions of the book's while the fitted curves, residuals and standard errors are not.
