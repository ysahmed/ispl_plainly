The marketing plan

Two pages, seven questions, no new machinery. The chapter opened by asking what a marketing manager would need to know before spending next year's budget — and this section collects every tool of Sections 3.1–3.3 and points it at those questions. It is the clearest demonstration in the book that a regression is not a number you compute once; it is a set of questions you ask in order.

Reading

  • Read Section 3.4 in full (pp. 109–111). Read it as a checklist: for each question, ask yourself which statistic answers this before reading the book's answer.
  • Skim Nothing — this section is the skim. It references Sections 3.1.2, 3.1.3, 3.2.2 and 3.3 without repeating them, so if any answer is unfamiliar, that is the gap to go back and fill.
  • Then Section 3.5 changes the subject entirely: what if you stop assuming a shape for the relationship at all?

Key ideas

Each of the seven questions maps to exactly one tool: is there relationship → F-test; how strong → RSE and R²; which media → t-test p-values; how large → confidence intervals; how accurately can we predict → confidence interval vs prediction interval; is it linear → residual plot; is there synergy → the interaction term's p-value and the jump in R². Three predictors, one model, seven distinct questions — and nothing outside Sections 3.1–3.3 was needed to answer any of them.

What you will know afterwards

  • which test answers which business question
  • RSE 1.69 vs mean 14.022 → ~12% error
  • newspaper: insignificant, not necessarily useless
  • confidence vs prediction, one last time
  • 90% → 97% of variance from one interaction term

2. What you would actually tell the client

Strip the notation and the seven answers collapse into four sentences:

  • Yes, advertising moves sales — the F-test is overwhelming (F = 570, p far below 0.001). Nobody needed statistics to believe this; the point is that the model can say it formally, which is what licenses everything that follows.
  • TV and radio are the two that carry it. Their p-values are both below 0.0001 and their confidence intervals — (0.043, 0.049) for TV and (0.172, 0.206) for radio — are narrow and nowhere near zero. Newspaper's is (−0.013, 0.011), straddling it, and its VIF of 1.145 says the problem is not collinearity: newspaper simply does not add anything once TV and radio are in the model. Judged alone (Table 3.3) it looked mildly useful — a reminder that "unrelated given the others" and "unrelated" are different claims.
  • The fit is good, and about 12% wrong. R² = 0.897 (the budgets explain almost 90% of the variation in sales) and RSE = 1.69 against a mean sale of 14.022 — a typical miss of 12%. For a planning model that is respectable; it is not precision.
  • Spend jointly, not separately. TV and radio reinforce each other: the interaction term has a small p-value and pushes R² from around 90% to almost 97%, meaning most of the structure the additive model was missing was exactly that interaction. Additive models are easy to interpret — and easy to be wrong with.

The pattern worth remembering

Every answer used machinery from Sections 3.1–3.3, and not one of them needed a new technique. Question 1 used the F-test, questions 2–4 used RSE/R² and t-tests, question 5 used the two intervals, question 6 used the residual plot, question 7 used an interaction term. If you can name which tool answers a given question, you have understood Chapter 3 — the algebra was only ever the delivery mechanism.

Section 3.4 in one line

Seven questions, seven statistics, one model: the whole value of linear regression is that you can hold it up against a business question and it gives you an answer you can check.
