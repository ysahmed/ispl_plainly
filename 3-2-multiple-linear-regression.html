Multiple linear regression

Three advertising budgets, one model, three coefficients. This section is where the book stops talking about "the relationship between x and y" and starts talking about this predictor's relationship with y after holding the others fixed — a phrase that carries almost all of the meaning in multiple regression, and the reason newspaper flips from useful to useless mid-section.

Reading

  • Read Section 3.2 in full (pp. 80–91). The four numbered questions in 3.2.2 are the checklist every regression analysis runs through; memorise the list, not the algebra.
  • Skim Forward, backward and mixed variable selection. Know what they do and why 2p makes them necessary; the clever versions arrive in Chapter 6.
  • Then Section 3.3 asks what happens when the predictors are categories, when the relationship bends, and when the assumptions underneath all of this quietly fail.

Key ideas

A multiple regression coefficient means the change in y for a one-unit change in this predictor, holding every other predictor fixed. Run the media separately and newspaper looks like it sells things (p = 0.00115); run them together and it does nothing (p = 0.86), because it was riding on radio's coattails. The F-test asks whether anything is related — one number that does not inflate with more predictors — while the individual t-tests say which. And a confidence interval about an average is always narrower than a prediction interval about one real outcome, because only the second has to absorb the noise.

What you will know afterwards

  • β_j: "holding the others fixed"
  • the least-squares surface and its floor
  • simple vs multiple coefficients (surrogates)
  • F-statistic vs individual t-statistics
  • R² never falls; adjusted R² might
  • confidence interval vs prediction interval

1. One equation, one slope per predictor (3.2.1)

Give every predictor its own coefficient and the model grows up without changing character:

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon$$

For the Advertising data that is sales = β₀ + β₁·TV + β₂·radio + β₃·newspaper + ε. The interpretation of βj gains the two words that do all the work: holding all other predictors fixed. β̂₂ = 0.189 says: spend an extra $1,000 on radio while keeping the TV and newspaper budgets the same, and sales rise by about 189 units. With two predictors the "line" is a plane (Figure 3.4), and with three it is a hyperplane — the point is that least squares still just minimises the same RSS over more coefficients.

The book declines to write the multiple-regression estimates out, because they need matrix algebra: β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy. What matters is the landscape: with one coefficient there was a parabola, with two there is a bowl-shaped surface whose floor sits at the least squares answer. Drag yourself around it:

2. Why not just run three separate regressions?

Because you would get three incompatible answers. Table 3.3 fits each medium on its own: TV +0.0475, radio +0.203, newspaper +0.055 (t = 3.30, p = 0.00115) — the newspaper coefficient clears the 1% bar, so a casual analyst would conclude newspaper advertising works. Put all three in one model (Table 3.4) and newspaper becomes −0.001 with p = 0.86: not merely insignificant, but signed backwards.

Both results are right; they answer different questions. The simple fit asks "do markets that spend more on newspaper sell more?" — and they do, because those markets also spend more on radio (correlation 0.35 in Table 3.5). The multiple fit asks "holding TV and radio fixed, does newspaper help?" — and it does not. Newspaper was a surrogate for radio, taking credit for someone else's effect. The book's absurd version: shark attacks correlate with ice cream sales until you control for temperature. Switch predictors off and watch it happen to real data:

"Holding fixed" is a modelling claim

You are not running an experiment; you are asserting that the model correctly represents how the predictors combine. If TV and radio interact (Section 3.3.2), then "the effect of TV, holding radio fixed" is not a single number at all — it depends on radio. The coefficient is always computable; whether it means what you think it means is the judgement part.

3. Questions one and two: anything there, and what (3.2.2)

Question 1 — is anything related? With p predictors the null hypothesis is a bundle: β₁ = β₂ = ⋯ = βp = 0. The tool is the F-statistic, a ratio of two averages:

$$F = \frac{(\mathrm{TSS} - \mathrm{RSS})/p}{\mathrm{RSS}/(n - p - 1)}$$

Read it as variance explained per predictor ÷ variance left over per leftover degree of freedom. If nothing is related, the two averages are both estimates of σ² and the ratio sits near 1. For the Advertising model F = 570 with a p-value essentially zero — overwhelming evidence that at least one medium is associated with sales. Note what F does not do: it does not get easier to impress as you add junk predictors, because the top of the fraction is divided by p.

Question 2 — which ones? The individual t-statistics in Table 3.4 answer "does this predictor help, given the others are already in?" — each one is exactly the F-test for dropping that one variable (t² = F for one degree of freedom). But the book warns twice about the trap: with 100 useless predictors, about five p-values land below 0.05 by chance, so "at least one is significant" tells you nothing unless you asked the F question first. When p grows, stop reading individual p-values and start selecting variables: forward (add whatever cuts RSS most), backward (drop whatever has the largest p-value), or mixed (both, until everything left is significant and everything out is not). All 2p subsets would be exhaustive — and for p = 30 that is over a billion models.

A significant F and non-significant predictors is a normal result

It means the predictors are jointly useful but you cannot currently tell which of them is carrying the load — often because they are correlated with each other (Section 3.3.3, collinearity) or because there simply is not enough data to separate them. The honest response is not "declare no effect"; it is "collect more data or simplify the model".

4. Questions three and four: how good, and what do we predict

Question 3 — fit. RSE and R² mean exactly what they meant in Section 3.1, computed the same way. Table 3.6 for the full model: RSE 1.69, R² 0.897, F 570. Compare with sales ~ TV alone (RSE 3.26, R² 0.612): adding radio nearly halves the typical miss. Two subtleties the section points out:

  • R² never decreases when you add a variable — RSS can only fall, because the old model is still available to the optimiser. So a tiny increase (0.89719 → 0.89721 for newspaper) is information: the new variable bought nothing.
  • RSE can increase. RSE divides by n − p − 1, so if the RSS gain is smaller than the cost of the extra coefficient, RSE goes up: TV+radio gives 1.681, all three gives 1.686. RSE charges for complexity; R² does not.

Numerical summaries also hide shape. Figure 3.5 plots the fit and shows it systematically overshooting markets that put everything into one medium and undershooting markets that split the budget — the additive model has no way to say "TV and radio together are worth more than the sum of the parts". Remember that pattern; Section 3.3.2 fixes it with one multiplied term.

Question 4 — prediction. Plugging values into ŷ = β̂₀ + β̂₁x₁ + ⋯ is easy. The hard part is naming your uncertainty, and the book counts three sources: the coefficients are estimated (reducible — a confidence interval), the linear form is an approximation (reducible — model bias), and the noise ε is irreducible (only a prediction interval absorbs it). The example: spend $100,000 on TV and $20,000 on radio in a city and the average sales across many such cities is in [10,985, 11,528] — while sales in this city are in [7,930, 14,580]. Same centre (11,256), radically different width.

Section 3.2 in one line

One model, one "holding the others fixed" reading per coefficient; an F-test for "is any of this real" before you go reading individual p-values; R² that always rises so let adjusted R² and RSE judge the fit; and two intervals — the narrow one about an average, the wide one about one actual outcome.
