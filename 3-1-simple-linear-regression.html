Simple linear regression

One predictor, one straight line. This section builds the whole apparatus — the line, the rule that picks it, the uncertainty around its slope, the p-value, and the two numbers that say whether the line is any good. Everything below is fitted live on the book's Advertising data, so the numbers you read in the text are the numbers the demos compute.

Reading

  • Read Section 3.1 in full (pp. 70–80). It is the densest ten pages of the chapter: every idea in the rest of the book — coefficients, standard errors, p-values, R² — is introduced here on one dataset.
  • Skim The closed-form estimates in equation (3.4). You will never compute them by hand; the point is only that a formula exists, so the fit is a number, not a judgement.
  • Then Section 3.2 adds the second and third predictors — and immediately shows why "run a separate regression for each one" is a trap.

Key ideas

Least squares picks the line whose squared misses add up to the least. That estimate is unbiased but noisy: another sample would give a slightly different slope, and SE(β̂₁) is how different. Divide the slope by its own standard error and you have a t-statistic; the p-value says how often noise alone would look that convincing. Finally, two scores: RSE in units of y (a typical miss, 3.26 = 23% of an average sale) and R² as a share of the variation (0.61 = just under two-thirds explained).

What you will know afterwards

  • Y ≈ β₀ + β₁X, and what β̂₀, β̂₁ mean
  • residuals → RSS → least squares
  • population line vs your line
  • SE, confidence interval, t, p-value
  • RSE: a miss in the units of y
  • R²: a share of the variation

1. The line, and the rule that picks it (3.1.1)

Start with the model in words: sales is approximately an intercept plus a slope times TV.

$$Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X$$

The book writes it with "≈" because the straight line is an approximation of a relationship we cannot see exactly. β₀ and β₁ are unknown constants — the model coefficients or parameters. Once we estimate them (the hat means "estimated"), any prediction is just arithmetic:

$$\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x$$

So how do we choose the estimates? Each candidate line misses each data point by a little — call that miss the residual, ei = yi − ŷi. Square every miss, add them up, and you have the residual sum of squares: the number the fit is minimising.

$$\mathrm{RSS} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_i\bigr)^2$$

Squaring is the whole decision: a miss of 10 costs 100, a miss of 2 costs 4 — so one bad point outweighs five small ones, which is both the power and the weakness of least squares (Section 3.3.4 comes back to it). Doing the calculus gives a closed form: β̂₁ is the ratio of the co-movement of x and y to the spread of x alone, and β̂₀ then puts the line through the point of averages (x̄, ȳ). On the Advertising data that produces β̂₀ = 7.03 and β̂₁ = 0.0475: an extra $1,000 of TV budget goes with about 47.5 more units sold.

Why minimise RSS at all?

It is a choice, and the book says so: "there are a number of ways of measuring closeness… by far the most common approach involves minimizing the least squares criterion." Chapter 6 swaps in other criteria (ridge, lasso) that shrink the coefficients instead of letting them wander. Least squares is the default because the estimates it produces are unbiased: over many datasets they average out to the truth.

2. Your line is one line among many (3.1.2)

The line you computed is the least squares line. Underneath it sits a line you will never see — the population regression line, the one implied by the true process Y = β₀ + β₁X + ε, where ε is everything the model leaves out: the non-linearity, the unmeasured variables, the measurement error. The book's simulated example makes the distinction visible: generate ten datasets from Y = 2 + 3X + ε and you get ten slightly different fitted lines around one unchanging truth.

So how far off is your line? The quantity that answers that is the standard error. For the sample mean it is the familiar √(σ²/n) — more data, tighter estimate. For the slope the formula has a second ingredient, and it is worth reading slowly:

$$\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}$$

The slope is better pinned down when the predictor values are spread out — you get more leverage on a slope when your observations are far apart in x. Since σ is unknown, it is replaced by the residual standard error, √(RSS/(n − 2)). Here is the same idea over and over: estimate, divide by your uncertainty, see how many standard errors you are from the null.

Two uses. First, a confidence interval: β̂₁ ± 2·SE(β̂₁) contains the true β₁ about 95% of the time across repeated samples. For the TV fit the book reports [0.042, 0.053] for β₁ and [6.130, 7.935] for β₀ — read as "each $1,000 of TV goes with between 42 and 53 more units sold, on average". Second, a hypothesis test:

$$t = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1 - 0}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat{\beta}_1)}$$

The null says there is no relationship at all (β₁ = 0). The t-statistic counts how many standard errors your estimate sits from zero; the p-value is the probability of seeing a number at least that large if the null were true. Table 3.1: TV's t is 17.67 — seventeen standard errors from nothing — with a p-value below 0.0001.

What a p-value is not

It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and not the probability that you made a mistake. It answers one narrow question: if there were truly no relationship, how surprising would these data be? A small p-value says "surprising"; it does not say "large" — a huge n makes a tiny, useless slope significant. Section 3.2.2 shows the opposite trap too: with many predictors, some p-values come out small by chance.

3. Two rulers for the fit: RSE and R² (3.1.3)

Rejecting "no relationship" only buys you the right to ask whether the line is good. Two numbers do the judging, and they are not interchangeable.

Residual standard error is the average distance between the data and the line, in units of y:

$$\mathrm{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-2}\mathrm{RSS}}$$

For sales on TV it is 3.26 — a typical miss of 3,260 units. Whether that is acceptable depends entirely on scale: average sales are 14,000 units, so the model is off by about 23% of a typical sale. That percentage is the honest way to quote an RSE.

R² throws away the units and reports a share instead: how much of the total wobble in y (TSS) the model removed (RSS):

$$R^2 = \frac{\mathrm{TSS} - \mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}$$

Here R² = 0.612: just under two-thirds of the variation in sales is explained by the TV budget. It runs from 0 to 1 regardless of whether y is measured in dollars or tons, which is why it travels so well between problems. And in this one-predictor setting it is exactly the squared correlation between x and y — a fact that stops being true the moment you have two predictors, which is one of the reasons R² exists.

Section 3.1 in one line

Least squares picks the line with the smallest squared misses; SE(β̂) tells you how much that line would wobble across samples; t and p turn the wobble into a yes/no about "any relationship at all"; and RSE and R² — one in units of y, one as a share — say whether the answer matters.
