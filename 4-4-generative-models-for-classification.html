Generative models for classification

Logistic regression asked "how probable is each class?" and modelled the answer directly. The methods here go the long way round: model what the predictors look like inside each class, then use Bayes' theorem to turn that around into class probabilities. Three flavours — LDA, QDA, naive Bayes — differ only in how much shape they assume.

Reading

  • Read Section 4.4 in full (pp. 146–160). The introduction and 4.4.1 are the concept; 4.4.2 is the longest stretch (confusion matrices and the ROC curve appear here and are used forever after); 4.4.3–4.4.4 are short variants.
  • Skim The density formulas (4.16, 4.23) — you need the idea (a bell curve per class), not the algebra. Keep Tables 4.6–4.7 (the jargon dictionary of rates) where you can find them.
  • Then Section 4.5 lines all these methods up against logistic regression and KNN to decide when each one wins.

Key ideas

A generative model estimates, for each class, the density of the predictors fk(x) and the class's prior share πk, then Bayes' theorem converts them to posterior probabilities. LDA assumes a bell curve per class with one shared covariance — boundaries are straight. QDA gives each class its own covariance — boundaries bend. Naive Bayes assumes the predictors are independent within each class — boundaries are made of added-up per-feature pieces. None is always best; the choice is bias–variance as always. Judging them needs new vocabulary: confusion matrix, sensitivity, specificity, threshold, ROC curve, AUC.

1. Turn Bayes' theorem around (4.4 intro)

Logistic regression models Pr(Y | X) — the answer directly. The generative approach models the two ingredients Pr(Y) and the distribution of X given Y, and lets Bayes' theorem assemble the answer:

$$p_k(x) = \Pr(Y = k \mid X = x) = \frac{\pi_k\, f_k(x)}{\sum_{l=1}^{K} \pi_l\, f_l(x)}$$
πk The prior: how common class k is before you see any predictors. Estimate it as the fraction of training rows in that class (Default: 0.0333 for "default").
fk(x) The density: how typical these predictor values are for class k — "a person with balance $2,400 is a rare breed among non-defaulters". This is the hard part to estimate: it is a full joint distribution over all p predictors.
pk(x) The posterior: the probability of class k after the evidence — what the classifier thresholds. Priors × evidence, divided by the total across all classes so the K numbers sum to 1.

Classify to the class with the largest posterior — that is the Bayes classifier from Section 2.2.3, the rule with the lowest possible error rate if the densities are correctly specified. So generative methods are in the business of guessing fk well.

Why bother with the detour when 4.3's logistic model is right there? The book gives three reasons: when classes are well separated, logistic's coefficient estimates become unstable while generative models stay steady; if the predictors really are bell-shaped within class and your sample is small, the extra assumption buys accuracy; and the framework extends naturally to K > 2 classes. Assumptions, paid for with assumptions — the usual trade.

2. LDA with one predictor (4.4.1)

With a single predictor, assume each class's values come from a normal (bell) curve with its own mean μk but a shared variance σ²:

$$X \mid Y = k \;\sim\; N(\mu_k, \sigma^2)$$

Drop those two bell curves into Bayes' theorem and the winning quantity per class — the discriminant function — comes out as

$$\delta_k(x) = \frac{x\,\mu_k}{\sigma^2} - \frac{\mu_k^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log \pi_k$$

and you assign x to the class with the largest δ. With two equally common classes the boundary collapses to one number:

$$x = \frac{\mu_1 + \mu_2}{2}$$

The midpoint of the two class means. That is the whole of one-dimensional LDA: measure where each group sits, cut in the middle (adjusting for unequal priors and using the shared spread to know how far apart is "far enough").

The book's Figure 4.4 works it out concretely: class means at −1.25 and +1.25, variance 1, 20 observations drawn from each class. The true (Bayes) boundary is at 0 with an irreducible error of 10.6% — the tails overlap, some mistakes are unavoidable. LDA, estimating from the 40 points, lands its boundary slightly off-centre and gets 11.1%: half a percent above the unattainable floor. The estimates behind it (4.20–4.21): μ̂k is just class k's sample mean; σ̂² pools the two within-class variances (weighting by nk − 1, dividing by n − K); π̂k = nk/n. Because δk(x) is a straight-line function of x, the method is linear discriminant analysis.

3. LDA with many predictors (4.4.2)

Real problems have p > 1 predictors, so we need a multivariate normal: a mean vector (the centre of the cloud) and a covariance matrix (its spread and tilt). The off-diagonal entries carry the association between predictors — Figure 4.5 shows the same cloud at correlation 0 (a round blob) and 0.7 (a tilted ellipse). LDA's assumption, once again, is that all K classes share one covariance matrix. Plugging into Bayes gives

$$\delta_k(x) = x^\mathsf{T} \Sigma^{-1} \mu_k - \tfrac{1}{2}\mu_k^\mathsf{T} \Sigma^{-1} \mu_k + \log \pi_k$$

— still a straight-line function of x (in two predictors: a line; in three: a plane; more: a flat slicing surface — that is the "linear" in LDA). Estimating needs K mean vectors plus one covariance matrix.

Figure 4.6 shows three classes with 20 observations each: three Bayes boundaries (one per pair of classes — line 1-vs-2, 1-vs-3, 2-vs-3) carving the space into three regions, and the LDA lines sitting nearly on top of them. Test error: Bayes 0.0746, LDA 0.0770 — LDA is doing well.

LDA on Default: 2.75% error, and why that's not the end of the story

Fit LDA to Default using balance and student status: training error 2.75%. The book refuses to let that number stand unqualified, with two caveats:

  • Training error is usually lower than test error — the real quantity of interest. Not a big worry here (p = 2, n = 10,000).
  • But also: 3.33% of the sample defaulted, so a null classifier that always predicts "no default" already achieves 3.33%. LDA's 2.75% beats it only "by a bit".

Read it as counts of mistakes, not rates. LDA flagged 104 people as defaulters: 81 were right, 23 were innocent — a tiny false-alarm rate (23 out of 9,667 non-defaulters). Sounds great, until the other diagonal: of the 333 real defaulters, 252 — 75.7% — were missed. The vocabulary for the two directions:

  • Sensitivity (recall): share of true defaulters correctly identified = 81/333 = 24.3%.
  • Specificity: share of non-defaulters correctly identified = 1 − 23/9667 = 99.8%.

Why the Bayes classifier is so lopsided here

LDA approximates the Bayes rule, and Bayes minimises the total number of errors — no matter which class they come from. With 3.3% defaulters, the cheapest way to minimise total errors is to be conservative about flagging "default". For a credit company hunting for risky customers, missing 76% of them may be unacceptable — so the company overrides Bayes and changes the threshold.

Move the threshold, trade one error for the other

The Bayes rule flags default when the posterior exceeds 0.5 (equation 4.26). Lower it — say flag when Pr(default | x) > 0.2 (4.27) — and the same fitted model makes more aggressive calls. Table 4.5 shows the new counts:

The book: that is a domain-knowledge question — it depends on what a missed defaulter costs versus an unnecessary phone call. Figure 4.7 draws the whole trade: overall error is lowest at threshold 0.5 (of course — that is Bayes), the defaulter-miss rate falls steadily as you lower the threshold, and the non-defaulter error rises.

The ROC curve: every threshold at once

The ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve sweeps the threshold from strict to reckless and plots the two rates against each other: true positive rate (= sensitivity) on the y-axis, false positive rate (= 1 − specificity) on the x-axis. The ideal classifier hugs the top-left corner; the diagonal is the "no information" guess. One number summarises it — the AUC, area under the curve. For LDA on Default the book reports AUC = 0.95 ("very good"; chance is 0.5), and notes logistic regression's ROC is virtually indistinguishable. Mixing them up is the classic mistake — "24.3% of defaulters are caught" and "78% of those flagged really default" are statements about different denominators.

4. QDA: one covariance per class (4.4.3)

Quadratic discriminant analysis keeps the bell curves and Bayes' theorem but drops the shared-covariance assumption: class k gets its own Σk. Its discriminant function

$$\delta_k(x) = -\tfrac{1}{2}(x - \mu_k)^\mathsf{T} \Sigma_k^{-1}(x - \mu_k) - \tfrac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma_k| + \log \pi_k$$

contains x multiplied by itself (the xTΣ−1x term), so the boundary between classes is a curve — hence "quadratic". Which is better? Bias–variance again, counted in parameters: with p predictors a covariance matrix has p(p+1)/2 entries, so QDA estimates K·p(p+1)/2 of them — with 50 predictors "some multiple of 1,275", says the book — while LDA shares one. LDA therefore has much lower variance; if the shared-spread assumption is badly wrong it pays in bias instead. Rule of thumb from the book: few training rows → LDA; very many rows, or clearly unequal spreads → QDA.

5. Naive Bayes: assume the predictors don't talk (4.4.4)

The third variant still uses Bayes' theorem, but attacks the hard part — estimating the joint density fk(x) — with a single cheeky assumption:

$$f_k(x) = f_{k1}(x_1) \times f_{k2}(x_2) \times \cdots \times f_{kp}(x_p)$$

"Within each class, the p predictors are independent." Instead of estimating one giant joint distribution (margins plus all the associations between predictors — the very thing covariance matrices capture for Gaussians), you estimate p separate one-dimensional distributions per class and multiply. The associations are not modelled; they are assumed away.

Do we believe it? The book is blunt: "In most settings, we do not." So why does the method exist? Bias–variance: the assumption adds bias but slashes variance — estimating a joint distribution needs huge amounts of data, and when n is modest relative to p that variance saving wins. "Naive Bayes is a good choice in a wide range of settings" precisely because its wrongness is cheap and its cheapness is valuable.

Each one-dimensional fkj can be estimated several ways: a Gaussian within the class (which quietly makes this "QDA with a diagonal covariance"), a histogram or smoothed kernel density (non-parametric), or — for a categorical predictor — simple proportions: if class k has 100 rows and its third predictor takes values 1/2/3 in 32/55/13 of them, estimate f̂ as 0.32/0.55/0.13.

The book's toy example shows the multiplication explicitly: p = 3 predictors (two quantitative, one three-level categorical), K = 2 classes, equal priors. For the new observation x* = (0.4, 1.5, 1):

  • class 1 densities: f̂₁₁(0.4) = 0.368, f̂₁₂(1.5) = 0.484, f̂₁₃(1) = 0.226 → product ≈ 0.0404
  • class 2 densities: f̂₂₁(0.4) = 0.030, f̂₂₂(1.5) = 0.130, f̂₂₃(1) = 0.616 → product ≈ 0.0024
  • posterior: Pr(Y = 1 | x*) = 0.944 vs 0.056 — a 94.4% vote for class 1 (Figure 4.10 draws all six density estimates).

And the honest verdict: with n = 10,000 and p = 2, the variance reduction isn't needed — expect naive Bayes to shine when p is larger or n smaller, where estimating the joint distribution is genuinely hard.

Section 4.4 in one line

Model the predictors per class and let Bayes' theorem do the reasoning: LDA shares one covariance (straight boundaries, low variance), QDA per-class covariances (bent boundaries, more parameters), naive Bayes tears out all correlation between predictors (cheap, biased, surprisingly strong on small samples). Judge any of them with a confusion matrix — sensitivity and specificity trade against each other as you move the threshold, and the ROC curve/AUC score the whole trade at once. Section 4.5 decides which method wins when.
