Step functions and basis functions

Section 7.1 let one curve bend. Section 7.2 refuses to draw a curve at all: it throws away the fact that age is continuous, sorts everyone into bins, and fits a flat constant in each one. It is a coarser model and it is deliberately so — nothing is assumed to happen inside a bin. Section 7.3 then points out that this and the polynomial you just met are the same trick wearing different clothes: pick a family of known functions, put them in a linear model, and fit them by least squares. The name of the family is the only decision left.

Reading

  • Read Sections 7.2 and 7.3 in full (pp. 293–294). They are two short pages: equations (7.4) to (7.7) and one figure between them.
  • Skim Equation (7.4). The indicator columns are the whole mechanism — once you accept that a column can be "is this observation in bin j?", everything else is the linear model of Chapter 3.
  • Then Section 7.4, which keeps the freedom to bend anywhere but insists the bends be smooth and joined. Step functions break; splines bend without breaking.

Key ideas

Cut the range of X into bins with cutpoints $c_1, \ldots, c_K$ and code membership as $K+1$ indicator columns — that is equation (7.4). Fit them by least squares and each bin's fitted value is that bin's own mean, while its coefficient is how far that bin sits above the first. Step functions impose no structure at all across bins, which is their freedom and their weakness. Then the generalization: any fixed, known family $b_1(X), \ldots, b_K(X)$ goes into $y = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j b_j(x) + \varepsilon$ and ordinary least squares applies unchanged, standard errors and F tests included. Polynomials are powers; step functions are indicators; splines, next section, are something better behaved. The fitted curve depends on the span of the basis, never on the coordinates you write it in — and this page shows that numerically rather than asserting it.

1. Cut the range, fit a constant

Choose cutpoints $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_K$ inside the range of $X$ and build one column per interval:

$$C_0(X) = I(X < c_1), \quad C_1(X) = I(c_1 \le X < c_2), \quad \ldots, \quad C_K(X) = I(c_K \le X) \qquad(7.4)$$

where $I(\cdot)$ is 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. Exactly one of these columns is 1 for any value of $X$ — they sum to 1 — so they divide the data into $K+1$ bins. Then fit

$$y_i \;=\; \beta_0 + \beta_1 C_1(x_i) + \beta_2 C_2(x_i) + \cdots + \beta_K C_K(x_i) + \varepsilon_i \qquad(7.5)$$

$C_0$ is left out because the intercept already carries it: when $X < c_1$ every predictor in (7.5) is zero, so $\beta_0$ is the mean response below the first cutpoint, and $\beta_j$ is how much higher bin $j$ sits than that first bin. Nothing else is assumed. The curve is not required to rise, to bend, or even to exist between two observations in different bins — it simply jumps.

At the default setting our four bin means are 94.1584, 116.5074, 118.9660 and 116.9398, and the first of them is the book's own lab number to every printed digit. The other three are not. The book's table prints 94.1584, 116.6608, 119.1887 and 116.5717, and the gap — at most four hundred dollars of annual salary — comes entirely from which side of a cutpoint a man aged exactly on it lands. Equation (7.4) writes the intervals as $c_1 \le X < c_2$, closing them on the left; pd.qcut closes them on the right, as $(c_1, c_2]$. Age is recorded in whole years, so 88 men are exactly 42 and 82 are exactly 51; those 170 men sit in a different bin under the two rules, and moving 88 salaries between bins moves a mean. Our bins hold 750, 671, 823 and 756 men; the lab's hold 750, 759, 817 and 674. We follow the equation, because that is what the page above prints — but you should know that the book's own figure was not built by following it either.

Switch the mode button to equal widths and the same three cutpoints become 33.5, 49 and 64.5. The fit is no worse on $R^2$ — 0.0625 against 0.0595 — but the bins now hold 750, 1,316, 862 and 72 men. That last number is the problem. A bin mean has standard error $\hat\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, so the bin holding 1,316 men has its level known to about 1.1 thousand dollars and the bin holding 72 has its known only to about 4.8 — four times worse — and it is that underpopulated top bin whose level draws the right-hand end of the curve. The quantile cutpoints cost nothing and keep the bins honest; that is why the lab uses them, and why 5-year age groups are the biostatisticians' default.

The book is candid about the weakness of all of this. Its own comment on Figure 7.2 is that "the first bin clearly misses the increasing trend of wage with age" — and you can see it in the panel above: the step from 94.16 to 116.51 is the model's entire account of everything between 18 and 34 years old.

2. The same bins, a yes-or-no answer

Section 7.1 asked whether a man earns more than $250,000 as a smooth function of age. Ask it again with bins:

$$\Pr(y_i > 250 \mid x_i) \;=\; \frac{\exp\!\big(\beta_0 + \beta_1 C_1(x_i) + \cdots + \beta_K C_K(x_i)\big)}{1 + \exp\!\big(\beta_0 + \beta_1 C_1(x_i) + \cdots + \beta_K C_K(x_i)\big)} \qquad(7.6)$$

At the default cutpoints the four bins contain 79 high earners split as 5, 21, 25 and 28, out of 750, 671, 823 and 756 men — fitted probabilities of 0.67%, 3.13%, 3.04% and 3.70%. The deviance falls from the null's 730.5 to 710.4: twenty units of deviance bought with four parameters, from a data set that contains 79 events. The degree-4 polynomial of the previous page reached 701.2. Neither is a large improvement, and that is the real finding — a man's age barely predicts whether he is in the top quarter-percent of earners, and no choice of basis will change that.

A bin is an average, and averages hide slopes

Step functions are enormously popular in biostatistics and epidemiology — the book says so, and offers 5-year age groups as its example: bins somebody else already decided on. The cost is visible in the left panel above: inside a bin the model claims wage does not change with age at all, so a genuine upward drift gets reported as a jump at each cutpoint, and the size of that jump depends on where you put the cut. Push the cutpoints slider to 1 and the whole of the trend between 18 and 80 is reduced to a single step at the median age of 42 — with the quantile cutpoints the default mode starts with.

3. One model, many families

Section 7.3 makes the general statement that both of the examples so far were special cases of. Fix a family of functions ahead of time — $b_1(X), b_2(X), \ldots, b_K(X)$ — and fit

$$y_i \;=\; \beta_0 + \beta_1 b_1(x_i) + \beta_2 b_2(x_i) + \cdots + \beta_K b_K(x_i) + \varepsilon_i \qquad(7.7)$$

The book's emphasis is on fixed and known: you choose the functions before you see the response, they never depend on $y$, and so the model is linear in its unknowns and least squares estimates them exactly as before. Everything Chapter 3 built — coefficient standard errors, confidence intervals, the F test for whether the whole family helps — is available with no modification. For polynomials $b_j(x) = x^j$; for step functions $b_j(x) = I(c_j \le x < c_{j+1})$. Wavelets or Fourier series would do as well. The next section picks the family this chapter is actually about.

Two things are worth watching while you press the buttons. The first is df: the slider counts basis columns, excluding the intercept, which is how equation (7.7) counts and how R counts a spline, so degree 4 in the powers family and 4 degrees of freedom in the natural-spline family both mean four columns plus an intercept — five coefficients each, in every family. What 4 does not mean is the same number of knots everywhere: the truncated-power cubic spends three of its columns on $x$, $x^2$ and $x^3$ and so reaches df 4 with a single interior knot, while the natural spline starts from one linear term and reaches df 4 with three. The readout prints the knot count beside the family name, so you never have to keep that in your head. The second thing is the RSS readout, which moves when you change families and does not move at all when you change coordinates. At df 4 the four families leave residual sums of squares of 4,771,604 for the powers, 4,773,100 for the truncated powers, 4,773,109 for the natural spline and 4,904,687 for the steps: the first three are within fifteen hundred of each other and the step function is a hundred and thirty thousand behind, which is section 7.2's weakness measured in the same units as everything else. They are different models, fitted in different spaces, and no amount of reparametrisation will make them agree. What reparametrisation does change is nothing you can see — two of these families can be checked against each other for free, because a cubic spline with no interior knots is a degree-3 polynomial and the two RSS readouts come out identical at 4,777,674. That is the whole claim of this section, and the max |difference| readout is there so that you never have to take it on trust.

Sections 7.2 and 7.3 in one line

A step function is a basis model whose basis is "which bin are you in", and a polynomial is a basis model whose basis is "which power of x is this". Both are fitted by the same least squares, both carry the same inference, and both are only as good as the family you chose. The fitted curve belongs to the span of the basis — the space of functions it can reach — and to nothing else. Two sets of columns that reach the same space produce the same curve to the last digit, which the readout above checks every time you press a button.

Source: ISLP, Sections 7.2 and 7.3 (pp. 293–294), Figure 7.2. Every curve, band, bin count and residual sum of squares on this page is fitted in your browser from the book's own Wage data set — 3,000 observations, ages 18 to 80, 79 of them above $250,000 — as shipped with the book's Python package. Nothing is simulated and no random number is drawn anywhere on this page. Two conventions are ours and are stated where they are used: equation (7.4) closes its intervals on the left while the lab's pd.qcut closes them on the right, so our bin means differ from the printed lab table by up to 0.4; and the four families in the last demo are all fitted with quantile cutpoints or quantile knots, the lab's choice, at the settings the slider shows. The basis columns are orthonormalised on the fly, so their signs and scales are ours — the fitted curves, residuals and standard errors are not.
