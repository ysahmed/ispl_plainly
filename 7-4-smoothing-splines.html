Smoothing splines

Section 7.4 made you choose the knots, and then choose how many of them. This section throws both questions away. Ask instead for the smoothest curve that fits the data well — write that demand down as one line of arithmetic, a loss plus a penalty on roughness — and the answer turns out to be something you have already met: a natural cubic spline with a knot at every distinct age, shrunk toward a straight line by a single number. One tuning parameter instead of two, and a formula that picks it without refitting anything.

Reading

  • Read Section 7.5 in full (pp. 300–303). Two short subsections: one defines the smoothing spline and describes what its answer looks like, the other chooses the tuning parameter. Three numbered equations — (7.11), (7.12), (7.13) — one unnumbered formula printed after them, and Figure 7.8 with two curves on it.
  • Skim Equation (7.11) itself, until the two terms and the knob between them are clear. Everything else in the section is a consequence of that single line: the shape of the answer, the matrix of smoother values, the count of effective degrees of freedom, and the leave-one-out shortcut.
  • Then Section 7.6, which stops fitting global functions of age altogether and estimates each point from its own neighbourhood.

Key ideas

The whole method is one equation: minimise the usual squared-error loss plus $\lambda \int g''(t)^2\,dt$, the squared second derivative of the fitted function, integrated over the predictor. Second derivative is roughness — zero for a straight line, large for something wiggly — so $\lambda$ is a dial between the best fit and the flattest curve. The book's result: the minimiser is a natural cubic spline with a knot at every distinct value of $x$, shrunk by $\lambda$; you never choose knots or their number again. The fitted values are one matrix, $S_\lambda$, times the response (7.12), and the sum of that matrix's diagonal, $\mathrm{df}_\lambda = \sum_i \{S_\lambda\}_{ii}$ (7.13), is what "$n$ effective degrees of freedom" means. $\lambda$ is chosen by leave-one-out cross-validation, which for a smoother costs about one fit: divide each residual by $1 - \{S_\lambda\}_{ii}$ and sum the squares.

1. Loss plus a penalty

Here is the whole method, and the book prints it as equation (7.11):

$$\min_g\;\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y_i - g(x_i)\bigr)^2 \;+\; \lambda \int g''(t)^2\,dt \qquad(7.11)$$

The first term is the one you have been using all book: how badly $g$ fits the points. The second is new. $g'$ is the slope of the curve at a place; $g''$ is the rate at which that slope is changing, which the book calls "a measure of its roughness: it is large in absolute value if $g(t)$ is very wiggly near $t$, and it is close to zero otherwise". Squaring it and integrating over the range of $t$ turns wiggles into a number, so that $\lambda$ can charge rent for them. The book's aside after that sentence is worth keeping: the second derivative of a straight line is zero — a perfectly smooth line costs nothing at all.

So the two extremes of $\lambda$ are forced, and the book states both. At $\lambda = 0$ the penalty is switched off, the fit is told only to fit, and the answer interpolates the data — "very jumpy". At $\lambda = \infty$ any wiggle is infinitely expensive, so the only surviving curve is one with no second derivative anywhere: the least squares straight line. Every value in between buys a compromise, and the demo below is that compromise on a dial, with the two ends drawn behind it in grey.

At the right-hand end the readouts print RSS 5,022,216 — and that is not a coincidence, it is the same fit. Page 7.2's basis demo prints exactly this number when its degrees-of-freedom slider sits at 1 in the powers family: the least squares straight line, which is what this method becomes as $\\lambda \\to \\infty$ — the second of the book's two forced limits, arrived at here from the other side. The left-hand end gives RSS 4,686,411 with roughness 82,898, and the entire span between the wiggliest answer this method can produce and the straightest one is 335,805 in residual sum of squares.

Two details in that left-hand end are worth stopping on, because they are where this data set quietly departs from the book's general statement. The book says there "will be a knot at each training observation, $x_1,\ldots,x_n$" and that effective degrees of freedom "decrease from $n$ to 2". Here $n$ is 3,000, but age takes only 61 distinct values between 18 and 80 — no man aged 78 or 79 appears in the table at all — so there are 59 interior knots and the top of the range is 61, not 3,000. And the $\lambda = 0$ fit cannot interpolate 3,000 points, because men of the same age earn different amounts: it hits all 61 age means exactly, and the RSS it leaves behind, 4,686,411, is nothing but the spread between men who share an age. No function of age alone can get under that number, and the demo's λ = 0 readout shows the fit's RSS landing on it to the last printed digit — the same figure that an ordinary natural spline with those 59 knots returns, which is the book's claim that the minimiser of (7.11) is a natural cubic spline, at $\lambda = 0$ without the shrinking.

The same readout prints one more number to file away: at $\lambda = 0$ the largest leverage, the largest single $\{S_\lambda\}_{ii}$, is 1.000000000000. One age in this table is occupied by exactly one man — he is 77 — and with no penalty to stop it the fit memorises him completely. Hold on to that until section 3.

2. Choosing λ: 6.8 degrees of freedom

If you do not choose knots, what do you choose? $\lambda$. And once $\lambda$ is the only decision left, the book needs two more pieces of machinery to describe it. The first is that the fit is linear in the response: the fitted values are one fixed matrix times $y$, $\hat{g}_\lambda = S_\lambda y$ (7.12), where $S_\lambda$ depends on the $x$'s and on $\lambda$ but not on the $y$'s. The second is the count that gives that matrix meaning: the effective degrees of freedom $\mathrm{df}_\lambda = \sum_i \{S_\lambda\}_{ii}$ (7.13), the sum of its diagonal. The book's summary is that as $\lambda$ runs from 0 to infinity these "decrease from $n$ to 2" — and on this data set, as the last section said, from 61 to 2, with 2 being the straight line and 61 the natural spline through all 61 age means.

Now pick $\lambda$. The book's suggestion is cross-validation, and its point is that leave-one-out is free here. Deleting one observation and refitting is normally $n$ separate fits; for a smoother it is one line of arithmetic:

$$\mathrm{RSS}_{\mathrm{cv}}(\lambda) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(y_i - \hat g_\lambda^{(-i)}(x_i)\bigr)^2 \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{y_i - \hat g_\lambda(x_i)}{1 - \{S_\lambda\}_{ii}}\right)^2$$

The left side is ordinary leave-one-out: fit everything except man $i$, see how badly that fit misses man $i$. The right side is the same number computed from the fit to all the data, by dividing his residual by $1$ minus his own leverage. Section 3 of this page checks that claim the slow way. For now, the consequence: search over $\lambda$, evaluate that sum, take the smallest, and you have Figure 7.8.

The book reports that leave-one-out chose a smoothing spline with 6.8 effective degrees of freedom. Ours comes out at 6.82 — the same decision, to the digit the book prints, from an implementation that never saw the book's code: a truncated-power natural spline basis at every distinct age, an exactly integrated roughness penalty, the objective solved as a ridge problem, and the criterion searched on the log scale. At that minimum the criterion reads 4,780,152, or a leave-one-out mean squared error of 1,593.38 — inside the band of ten-fold values page 7.3 printed for its splines (1,591.5 to 1,593.8), which is about as much agreement as two different validation schemes ever show.

The book also says of its two curves that there is "little discernible difference" between them and that the 16-degree-of-freedom one "seems slightly wigglier". Both halves of that are numbers, so the demo prints them. The largest gap anywhere between the red and blue curves is 5.2 thousand dollars (at age 70), and the mean gap over the whole age range is 1.7 — on wages running from about 60 to 130, that is the "little discernible difference". And the wiggling has a price that RSS barely shows: at df 16 the red curve's RSS is 4,747,460 against the blue curve's 4,761,557, a difference of 0.3 percent, while its roughness $\int g''^2$ is 25.6 against 1.27 — twenty times the wiggle, bought with a third of a percent of fit. That is the overfitting of section 7.1 in a new outfit.

The right-hand panel is worth reading slowly, because it is the third time this book has shown you a cross-validation curve and every time it has been flat. The criterion falls 248,553 on the way into its minimum — from 5,028,705 at the straight line to 4,780,152 at df 6.82 — and then climbs only 83,546 over the next fifty-three degrees of freedom, reaching 4,863,698 at df 60. Expressed as percentages of the minimum: df 12 is 0.11 percent above it, df 30 is 0.68 percent, df 56 is 1.49 percent. The straight line, at 5.20 percent, is genuinely too stiff — this is the one place in the chapter where the validation curve rejects an endpoint decisively — but almost anything between about 5 and 40 degrees of freedom would have told you a story that matches the data about equally well.

One loose end, and the previous section predicted it. At exactly df 61 the criterion does not have a minimum to speak of: it has a division by zero. That is the 77-year-old whose leverage at $\lambda = 0$ is exactly 1.000000000000 — one observation at an age all his own, fitted perfectly, so $1 - \{S_\lambda\}_{ii}$ vanishes and the formula asks for his residual over zero. The search never goes there, but the right-hand panel does run right up to it, and the last few points before df 61 are the criterion beginning to climb.

3. The shortcut, checked

The leave-one-out formula is doing something that deserves suspicion. It says that the fit you would have got had man $i$ never existed is available from the fit that did include him — simply by dividing his residual by $1$ minus his leverage. The book's phrasing is that it lets you "compute each of these leave-one-out fits using only $\hat{g}_\lambda$, the original fit to all of the training observations". That is an exact algebraic claim, and this site tests exact claims by doing the expensive thing anyway.

So the demo below deletes the first man at each of the 61 distinct ages, one at a time, and for each of them solves the $61\times61$ normal equations again from scratch with that single observation's row removed — same knots, same $\lambda$, one rank-one piece of $Z'Z$ and $Z'y$ gone. Then it compares the book's shortcut against the answer the fresh solve gives.

The answer, at the default 16 degrees of freedom: the largest disagreement over all 61 deletions is 1.8 × 10−13 thousand dollars, and both routes produce the same leave-one-out sum of squares, 115,491.5868, to every digit printed. In ordinary money that worst disagreement is under two ten-billionths of a dollar. The same check at df 3, at the cross-validated 6.8 and at df 40 stays below 2 × 10−11 — the size of rounding in double precision, not the size of an approximation. The formula is exact, and the demo re-proves it every time you move the slider.

The right-hand panel shows the mechanism, and it is worth a sentence because it is the whole content of the formula. A residual $y_i - \hat g_\lambda(x_i)$ from the full fit is optimistic: man $i$ was part of the fit that judged him, and his own leverage $\{S_\lambda\}_{ii}$ is how much of that judgment he contributed. Dividing by $1 - \{S_\lambda\}_{ii}$ inflates his residual to what it would have been without him, and the largest ÷(1 − Sii) readout is the biggest such correction in the sample: 1.2314 at df 16, meaning the worst-affected man's residual grows by 23 percent. At df 3 it is 1.0173 and the residuals are barely touched, because a stiff fit spreads its weight evenly and leans on no one man; at df 40 it is 1.4178, because a fit that flexible is well on its way to memorising each observation. That ratio is also the reason the criterion has a hole at df 61: wherever the leverage reaches 1, the correction is a division by zero.

Section 7.5 in one line

Throw away the knots and pay for wiggle instead: the function that minimises squared error plus $\lambda$ times roughness is a natural cubic spline with a knot at every distinct value of the predictor, shrunk toward a straight line by $\lambda$; the sum of its smoother matrix's diagonal is what "$n$ effective degrees of freedom" means; and leave-one-out picks $\lambda$ by dividing each residual by one minus its leverage, which this page checked against 61 genuine refits that never disagreed by more than a hundred-billionth of a dollar.

Source: ISLP, Section 7.5 (pp. 300–303), Equation (7.11) to (7.13), the leave-one-out formula printed after (7.13), and Figure 7.8. Every curve, residual sum of squares, roughness penalty, effective degree of freedom and leave-one-out criterion on this page is fitted in your browser from the book's own Wage data set — 3,000 observations, ages 18 to 80, 79 of them above $250,000 — as shipped with the book's Python package. Nothing is simulated, and no random number is drawn anywhere on this page: the 61 men checked in the third demo are the first in the table at each distinct age. Four conventions are ours and are stated where they are used: the knots are placed at every distinct age, which is the book's own rule and gives 59 interior knots between the boundary ages 18 and 80, so effective degrees of freedom run from 61 to 2 on this data rather than from $n$ to 2; the roughness penalty $\int g''(t)^2\,dt$ is integrated over the observed age range, where $g''$ lives; the objective is solved in an orthonormalised basis and $\lambda$ is searched on a log scale, which changes the units of $\lambda$ but not the fitted curve, the degrees of freedom or the cross-validated choice; and Figures 7.8's two curves are ours, fitted on all 3,000 rows, since the book does not say how many rows its figure used.
