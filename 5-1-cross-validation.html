Cross-validation

You have fitted a model. You know how it does on the data it saw — the question that actually matters is how it does on data it never saw, and nobody hands you a set of those. This section builds the three tricks for getting an honest answer out of the one dataset you have: hold out a chunk, hold out one point at a time, or hold out a handful of groups. The demos below do all of it live on the book's 392 cars, so every curve is one your browser just computed.

Reading

  • Read Section 5.1 in full (pp. 202–212). It is the longest section in the chapter: five subsections, each one a refinement of the last.
  • Skim The bias-variance argument in 5.1.4 if you already met it in Chapter 2 — the new part is only that the estimate of the test error has a bias and a variance of its own.
  • Then Section 5.2, which is short: the bootstrap, the other half of the resampling toolbox.

Key ideas

The training error is computed on the very data that chose the fit, so it is always optimistic — often wildly so. The test error is what you care about, and you can only estimate it with data the fit did not see. So: pretend part of your data is a test set. The validation set approach holds out half (noisy, and the model trains on less data). LOOCV holds out one point at a time (honest, almost no wasted data, but n fits — though for least squares a formula does it in one). k-fold CV with k = 5 or 10 holds out k groups in turn: the compromise everyone actually uses. For classification the score changes from squared error to a simple count of mistakes; everything else stays.

1. The question you cannot answer with training error

Chapter 2 already showed the shape of the problem: fit anything flexible enough and its training error falls, and keeps falling, even when the relationship it is chasing is pure noise. The training error answers the wrong question — "how well does this model describe the data it was handed?" — while you are asking "how well will it do next time?".

The book puts the ideal version plainly: if you had a large designated test set, you could just use it. You almost never do. So the rest of the section invents one out of the training data: split what you have, fit on one part, score on the other, and repeat the split in whatever pattern gives the most trustworthy average.

Two numbers, one dataset

Every method on this page follows the same three steps — split the data into a part that fits and a part that scores, fit on the fitting part, score on the scoring part — then average the scores over however many splits the method calls for. The three methods differ only in how they choose the splits.

2. The validation set approach

Randomly cut the data in half. Fit on the first half. Predict the second half and take its mean squared error. That number estimates the test error, and the demo below starts in exactly that mode — press the buttons to see the other two splittings the section is about to introduce (they share one picture because they are one idea with three settings).

Now the real thing. The book runs this on the Auto data: predict mpg from polynomial functions of horsepower, degrees 1 through 10, split the 392 cars in half, and look at the validation error for each degree. The quadratic fit beats the linear one by a mile; the cubic is slightly worse than the quadratic — so the extra term is not buying anything. That is the whole promise of the method: you answered "is degree 3 worth it?" without ever looking at a test set.

Which brings us to the method's two drawbacks, exactly as the book lists them:

  • The answer depends on the draw. Reshuffle and you get a visibly different estimate — right panel above. The estimate of the test error is itself a random variable, and with only two groups it is a very twitchy one.
  • The model is trained on less data. Half. Models improve with more training data, so a model fitted on 196 cars is scored, then judged as if it had been fitted on all 392 — which makes the validation error run a little high compared to the real thing.

Safe to skip on a first read

Whether the validation error over- or under-shoots depends on two opposing forces (less training data pushes it up; scoring on data the model never saw pushes it down). The book keeps the practical version: treat it as an estimate, and prefer methods below whose averages are steadier.

3. Leave-one-out cross-validation

Fix the first drawback by holding out a single observation: fit on the other n − 1, predict the one you kept, take that squared error — then move on to the next observation and do it again. After n runs you have n squared errors, and their average is the LOOCV estimate of the test MSE:

$$\mathrm{CV}(n) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{MSE}_i$$

Read as a sentence: the leave-one-out estimate is one over n, times the sum of the n errors, where error i is the miss on the single observation that was left out of fit i.

Two things went right. Every fit used n − 1 points — nearly all the data — so the second drawback is gone. And there is nothing random in the split: run it twice and you get the same number to the last digit, so the first drawback is gone too. The cost is n fits of the model, which for something slow is a real price.

For least squares, though, it is not a price at all. An amazing shortcut exists: fit the model once, then

$$\mathrm{CV}(n) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(\frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{1 - h_i}\right)^2$$

Read as a sentence: each residual is divided by one minus that observation's leverage hi before it is squared and averaged. Leverage (from equation 3.37) measures how much observation i pulled the fit towards itself — it runs from 1/n for an ordinary point up towards 1 for a point sitting far from the rest. Dividing by (1 − h) inflates the misses of exactly the points the fit was most able to chase, which is the correction you would have gotten by refitting without them.

Section 5.1.2 in one line

LOOCV is the validation idea with the arbitrariness removed — every point gets one turn as the test set — and for least squares it costs one fit plus a division by (1 − h) per point.

4. k-fold cross-validation

n fits is only free for least squares. For a method whose fitting procedure is slow, the way out is to split the data into k groups — "folds" — of about equal size, and use each fold once as the validation set while fitting on the other k − 1:

$$\mathrm{CV}(k) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathrm{MSE}_i$$

Read as a sentence: the k-fold estimate is the average of the k fold-by-fold errors — one per fold, so k fits total. Set k = n and you have rediscovered LOOCV; in practice people use k = 5 or k = 10, which means 5 or 10 fits no matter how big the dataset is.

There is also a statistical bonus. Section 5.1.1's first drawback was variability, and 5.1.3's right-hand panel is the answer: nine different 10-fold runs of the same calculation land close together, much closer than nine validation-set splits do. 5. Which k is better? The bias-variance trade-off, again

Chapter 2's trade-off was about models. This one is about the error estimate itself: every CV number you compute is a random variable with its own bias (does it sit systematically above or below the truth?) and its own variance (does it jump around?). The book argues the two sides pull against each other as k changes:

  • Bias. The validation approach trains on half the data, so its estimate runs high. LOOCV trains on n − 1 points, so it is almost unbiased — from the book's own words, it is to be preferred "from the perspective of bias reduction". k = 5 or 10 sits in between: each fit sees (k − 1)n/k points, so a touch of bias, but far less than a half-split.
  • Variance — the book's argument. LOOCV averages the outputs of n fits trained on almost identical data, so those outputs are strongly correlated, and averaging correlated quantities steadies an estimate much less than averaging independent ones. k-fold averages k outputs from fits that overlap less, so it steadies better. On that reasoning the book states that LOOCV's estimate carries higher variance than k-fold's.
  • So: k = 5 or 10 — estimates that "suffer neither from excessively high bias nor from very high variance", at a cost of only k fits.

Only simulation can check any of this, because it needs the true test error. The demo simulates data from a known truth, repeats the whole experiment 40 times, and reports each flavour's average error (its bias) and its spread across the repeats (its variance) at the best degree.

That is the section's conclusion: pick k = 5 or k = 10. Empirically they give estimates that suffer neither from high bias nor from very high variance — and they are cheap.

Section 5 in one line, so far

Never score a model on the data that fitted it. Hold something back — half, one point, or k folds — fit on the rest, score what you held back, average. Prefer k = 10 unless each fit is very expensive, in which case k = 5, and only fall back on a single validation split when the data is too large to do better.

6. Cross-validation on classification problems

Nothing about splitting changes when Y is a label rather than a number — only the score. Instead of mean squared error, count the mistakes:

$$\mathrm{CV}(n) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Err}_i, \qquad \mathrm{Err}_i = I(y_i \neq \hat{y}_i)$$

Read as a sentence: the CV error rate is the average of the n hold-out errors, where each error is 1 if the prediction was wrong and 0 if it was right. The validation and k-fold versions replace the divisor with 2 or k and the terms with each group's proportion of mistakes — same machinery, different ruler.

The book's example is the two-dimensional simulated classification data from Figure 2.13, fitted with logistic regressions on polynomial terms of increasing degree. In the simulation you know the truth: the test error rates for degrees 1 to 4 are 0.201, 0.197, 0.160 and 0.162, against a Bayes error rate of 0.133 — no classifier can do better than that last number. In real data you know none of this. So the book fits ten logistic regressions (degrees 1–10), computes the 10-fold CV error for each, and reads the answer off the CV curve: it reaches its minimum at degree 4, right next to the true minimum at degree 3, while tracking the true test error closely throughout. In the book's run the CV curve sits a touch under the test curve; in the demo below it sits a touch over. Which side it lands on is sampling noise — what matters is that the two curves move together, and that the training error, which only ever falls, does not.

Section 5.1 in one line

Cross-validation is the honest way to estimate how a model will do on the next batch of data: split, fit, score, average — with k = 10 as the default, the (1 − h) formula when the model is least squares, and an error count instead of squared error when the response is a label. It can never tell you the answer for data drawn from a different world, only for this one held back.
