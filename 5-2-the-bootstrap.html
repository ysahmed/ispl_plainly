The bootstrap

Cross-validation answers "how well will this model predict?". The bootstrap answers a different question: "if I had drawn a different sample, how much would this number have moved?" — that is, how uncertain is an estimate. The trick is old but the framing is the point of this section: you cannot go back to the population for a second sample, so you pretend your own dataset is the population and sample from it, with replacement, over and over.

Reading

  • Stare at Figure 5.10: the left histogram needs a population to exist, the middle one needs only one dataset. The fact that they look alike is the whole argument.

Key ideas

A standard error tells you how much an estimate would vary across samples. Textbook formulas provide one for familiar quantities (Chapter 3's SE of a regression slope), but not for most of the methods you will actually use. The bootstrap replaces the missing second sample: draw n observations from your data with replacement, redo the entire calculation, and repeat B times (B = 1,000 or more). The spread of the B answers estimates the spread you would get from B fresh samples — no formulas, no assumptions, works for any method that can be run twice. The book's toy example is an investment allocation α; the payoff is bootstrap standard errors for a regression fit sitting right on top of the analytic ones.

What you will know afterwards

  • sampling with replacement = a fake second dataset
  • B resamples → a whole distribution of the estimate
  • SE = the standard deviation of those B answers
  • why fresh samples and bootstrap samples agree
  • α: estimating a quantity nobody gave a formula for
  • bootstrap SE of a slope vs Chapter 3's formula
  • what to do when the resample is degenerate

1. The problem: you only get one sample

Suppose you compute some statistic from your data — a mean, a correlation, a fitted coefficient, an allocation rule. The natural question is: how much would that statistic move if the study were repeated with a new sample from the same population? For a handful of classical statistics, mathematics answers this in advance and software prints the standard error with the estimate.

But as the book says, the power of the bootstrap lies in it being applicable to a wide range of methods — including ones for which nobody has ever derived a formula, and including complicated procedures where "the standard error of the output" is not even well defined until you decide what to wiggle. In practice the procedure is always the same three lines:

  • Resample: draw n observations from your data with replacement, so some rows appear twice, some not at all.
  • Recompute: run your entire calculation on that bootstrap sample, whatever it is.
  • Repeat B times, then look at the spread of the B results.

"With replacement" in one example

Your data has rows 1 through 100. A bootstrap sample is also 100 rows long, drawn by rolling a 100-sided die 100 times: row 43 might come up three times, row 7 never. The size stays n; the membership churns. Every time a row repeats, its X and Y travel together.

2. The toy example: choosing an investment allocation

You have a fixed sum to invest in two assets with returns X and Y, putting fraction α in X and (1 − α) in Y. You want the α that makes the variance of the combined return smallest. The book derives it:

$$\alpha = \frac{\sigma_Y^2 - \sigma_{XY}}{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - 2\sigma_{XY}}$$

Read as a sentence: alpha equals Y's variance minus the covariance, divided by X's variance plus Y's variance minus twice the covariance.

Those three population quantities are unknown, so you estimate them from your 100 past returns and plug them in — giving α̂, the sample-based answer:

$$\hat{\alpha} = \frac{\hat{\sigma}_Y^2 - \hat{\sigma}_{XY}} {\hat{\sigma}_X^2 + \hat{\sigma}_Y^2 - 2\hat{\sigma}_{XY}}$$

Same formula with hats on: the estimates computed from the data instead of the unknowable truth. The demo below evaluates it on the book's Portfolio data right now — the left panel shows the 100 returns and the three plug-in quantities the readout quotes.

How accurate is α̂? The book first answers the idealized way: it knows the simulation's parameters (σX² = 1, σY² = 1.25, σXY = 0.5, so the true α is 0.6), so it can draw 1,000 completely new datasets from that population, compute α̂ on each, and look at the spread. The 1,000 estimates average 0.5996 with a standard deviation of 0.083 — so α̂ typically misses α by about 0.08. That is the answer, and it needs a population to sample from, which real data does not come with.

3. What the bootstrap does instead

Instead of going back to the population, take your single dataset and make B copies of it, each by resampling n rows with replacement. Each copy produces its own α̂*; the spread of those B estimates estimates the spread of the real thing. The book illustrates the mechanics on a dataset with only three observations, Z, because with n = 3 you can see every moving part:

$$\mathrm{SE}_B(\hat{\alpha}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B-1} \sum_{r=1}^{B} \left(\hat{\alpha}^*_r - \frac{1}{B}\sum_{s=1}^{B} \hat{\alpha}^*_s\right)^2}$$

Read as a sentence: take the B bootstrap estimates, subtract their average, square, average those squares with a B − 1 divisor, and take the square root — the ordinary standard deviation formula, applied to the bootstrap answers instead of raw observations.

Why should bootstrap spread match fresh-sample spread at all? The book's evidence is the comparison you just watched: the histogram of 1,000 bootstrap estimates from one dataset looks like the histogram of 1,000 fresh samples from the population, and their standard deviations land close (0.087 against 0.083 in the book's run). The resample reproduces the variation the population would have imposed, because each bootstrap sample is a plausible version of "what the data might have looked like".

What the bootstrap does not do

It estimates the variability of an estimate under repeated sampling from this same population. It cannot tell you what happens if the world changes — a different population, a different decade — and it inherits every bias your method already has. A bootstrap spread around a badly biased estimator is a precise answer to the wrong question.

4. Bootstrap standard errors for a regression fit

Section 5.2 opens by pointing at the simplest useful target: the standard errors of the coefficients of a linear regression. Chapter 3 derived those in closed form — but only for least squares. The demo below runs both roads on the same fit (Auto, mpg against horsepower) and puts them side by side.

The analytic route is Chapter 3's: fit once, and the SE of the slope comes out of the residual variance and the spread of the predictor. The bootstrap route resamples the 392 cars B times with replacement, refits on each sample, and reads the standard deviation of the B slopes off the histogram. If the two agree, the machine works — and from now on you can point the same machine at any method that has no formula.

Section 5.2 in one line

When you want to know how stable a number is and no formula exists, make yourself more datasets: resample what you have with replacement, B times, redo the whole calculation each time, and read the standard deviation of the answers. It is Chapter 3's standard error, obtained by brute force instead of algebra — and it works for methods that have no algebra.
