Generalized linear models

Categories were one way to break linear regression. Counts are the other: how many bikers at 2 a.m. — a number that cannot be negative, cannot be fractional in principle, and whose noise grows with the size of the answer. The fix, Poisson regression, turns out to be the third member of a family you have already met: linear and logistic regression are in it too.

Reading

  • Read Section 4.6 in full (pp. 167–172). 4.6.1 lists what goes wrong (read all three failures); 4.6.2 is the Poisson fix; 4.6.3 is the two-page generalisation that names the whole family.
  • Skim The Poisson likelihood (4.38) — it is the maximum-likelihood trick from 4.3 again with a different probability formula. The coefficient tables are for skimming too; the ideas matter more than the digits.
  • Then Section 4.7's lab fits all of Chapter 4's methods in Python (when it is written); otherwise Chapter 5, which is about measuring accuracy honestly.

Key ideas

Counts are neither categories nor continuous numbers, and linear regression fails them three ways: negative predictions (~10% of the Bikeshare fits), variance that grows with the mean (busy hours are noisier hours — the model assumes constant spread), and a continuous error term on an integer response. The Poisson distribution models counts with one parameter λ that is both the mean and the variance, and Poisson regression lets log(λ) be a straight line in the predictors — non-negative by construction, coefficients read as multiplicative factors. Linear, logistic and Poisson regression are then revealed as one recipe: choose a distribution, choose a link function, make the linked mean linear. That recipe is a generalized linear model.

1. A response that is a count

The Bikeshare data: the response bikers is the number of people using a Washington, DC bike-sharing program in a given hour. Predictors: mnth (month), hr (hour, 0–23), workingday (1 if the day is neither a weekend nor a holiday), temp (normalised temperature), and weathersit — one of four levels: clear; misty or cloudy; light rain or snow; heavy rain or snow. The book treats mnth, hr and weathersit as qualitative.

Neither Chapter 3 nor Chapter 4 fits: the response is not a category, and it is not an ordinary number either. So what happens if we just run linear regression anyway? Section 4.6.1 shows the results and then the problems.

2. Linear regression on Bikeshare — and its three failures (4.6.1)

The fit looks plausible (Table 4.10): temperature +157 bikers per unit; cloudy weather −12.89; light rain −66.49; heavy rain −109.75 (not significant — very few such hours); workingday +1.27 with p = 0.48, i.e. nothing. The month and hour coefficients (Figure 4.13) even make sense: highest in spring and autumn, lowest in winter, peaks at commuting hours, trough overnight. "At first glance… reasonable and intuitive," says the book. Then:

  1. Negative predictions. 9.6% of the fitted values are below zero — the model forecasts minus people on a bike. Predictions like that undermine the coefficients, confidence intervals and everything else the model reports.
  2. The variance is not constant. When few people are expected, few actually show up (December, 1–4 a.m., raining: mean 5.05 bikers, sd 3.73); when many are expected, the numbers swing wildly (April–June, 7–10 a.m., clear: mean 243.59, sd 131.7). The mean moves, and the spread moves with it — but the linear model assumes one constant σ² for everyone. That mismatch (heteroscedasticity) breaks its assumptions.
  3. Integer response, continuous error. The model says Y = (sum of terms) + ε with ε continuous — so Y is necessarily continuous. Counts are not.

The quick fix the book considers: regress log(Y) instead. That removes negative predictions (log of a count is fine) and tames much of the spread problem — but interpretation becomes "a unit of X raises the mean of the log of Y by β", and the transform dies outright when Y can be 0. Better to change the model, not the response.

3. Poisson regression (4.6.2)

Start with the Poisson distribution — the distribution of counts:

$$\Pr(Y = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}, \quad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$

One parameter, λ > 0, and its two roles are the heart of the idea:

$$\lambda = E(Y) = \mathrm{Var}(Y)$$

The mean equals the variance. Quiet hours are quiet and predictable; busy hours are busy and unpredictable — exactly failure 2, written as a model. The book's λ = 5 worked example: Pr(no users) = e−5 = 0.0067, Pr(1) = 0.034, Pr(2) = 0.084. Drag λ yourself and watch the mean readout and the variance readout stay locked together:

Let the mean follow the predictors — through a log

A fixed λ is boring; we want λ to depend on hour, weather and month. The model (4.36–4.37):

$$\log\bigl(\lambda(X_1, \ldots, X_p)\bigr) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda(X) = e^{\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p}$$

Take the log of the mean — not of the response this time — and make that linear. Two consequences fall out for free: λ = e… can never be negative (failure 1 gone), and because the model is built on the Poisson distribution, the mean-variance lock stays in place (failure 2 gone). Estimation is the familiar maximum likelihood of 4.3: multiply the fitted Poisson probabilities of the counts you actually saw, maximise over the β̂.

Fit it to Bikeshare (Table 4.11) and the story is qualitatively the same as linear regression — same seasons, same rush hours, temperature up, weather down — with three distinctions the book draws:

Linear regressionPoisson regression
Coefficient meaning β = added bikers, one-for-one eβ = factor on the mean: cloudy is ×0.92 of clear (e−0.08 = 0.923); rain multiplies a further ×0.61 (e−0.5 = 0.607), i.e. rainy hours run at about 56% of clear-hour usage
Variance constant σ² — wrong for this data Var = mean by construction — busy hours are allowed to be noisier

Small but interesting: workingday is statistically significant under the Poisson model (z = 7.5) yet not under the linear model (p = 0.48) — changing the variance assumption changed what the data could say. And the book's footnote to keep in mind: the Bikeshare variance is actually much higher than the mean — a condition called overdispersion that inflates the z-statistics in Table 4.11. Correcting it needs methods beyond this book; just know the printed z's are optimistic.

4. The general recipe: generalized linear models (4.6.3)

Step back and the three models of this book so far share one skeleton. The book lays it out as two numbered ingredients plus the link:

  1. A distribution for Y given X. Linear regression assumes Gaussian; logistic assumes Bernoulli (success/failure); Poisson assumes Poisson (counts).
  2. The mean of Y as a function of the predictors. Linear: E(Y | X) = β₀ + β₁X₁ + … (a straight line). Logistic: E(Y | X) = eβ·X / (1 + eβ·X) (the sigmoid — it is Pr(Y = 1 | X), since a Bernoulli's mean is its probability). Poisson: E(Y | X) = eβ·X.
  3. The link function η — a transformation that makes the mean linear: η(E(Y | X)) = β₀ + β₁X₁ + … The three links you have implicitly been using all along:
    ModelLink η(μ)What it does
    Linearlog nothing: η(μ) = μ (identity)mean already linear — no transform
    Logisticη(μ) = log(μ / (1 − μ)) (logit)compresses the (0, 1) probability onto the whole real line
    Poissonη(μ) = log(μ)compresses the positive mean onto the whole real line

So 4.3's logit equation and 4.6.2's log-λ equation are the same equation with a different link — and Chapter 3's straight line is that equation with the identity link, i.e. no transform at all. The Gaussian, Bernoulli and Poisson distributions all belong to one larger club, the exponential family (which also contains the exponential, Gamma and negative binomial distributions — Gamma and negative binomial regression exist too, though not in this book).

The definition, in one paragraph

Pick a distribution family for your response. Pick a link function that maps its mean onto the real line. Declare the linked mean to be a straight line in the predictors. Fit by maximum likelihood. Any method following that recipe is a generalized linear model (GLM) — so linear regression, logistic regression and Poisson regression are not three unrelated techniques but three settings of one machine: change the distribution, change the link, everything else (coefficients, z-statistics, likelihood thinking) carries over. That is how Chapter 4 ends: categories were never a special case, and neither were counts — they were link functions.
