Why not linear regression?

The obvious shortcut: turn the categories into numbers and reuse Chapter 3. This short section kills the idea properly — for more than two classes the numbers make up an order that does not exist, and even for two classes the straight line happily reports a "probability" of −12% or 140%.

Reading

  • Read Section 4.2 in full (pp. 136–137). Two pages, one example, one picture — and Figure 4.2 is worth pausing on: it shows the same Default data fitted both ways.
  • Skim The aside that linear regression's classifications for two classes happen to match LDA's. It is a curiosity now; Section 4.4 explains why later.
  • Then Section 4.3 builds the fix: a model that is guaranteed to output probabilities.

Key ideas

Encoding categories as 1, 2, 3 pretends they are ordered and evenly spaced — a second modeler gets a completely different answer by coding them differently. With two classes, 0/1 coding almost works: the fitted line does estimate the probability of class 1 — but nothing stops it from leaving the [0, 1] range where probabilities must live. The book's summary: regression (a) cannot accommodate more than two classes at all and (b) does not give meaningful estimates of Pr(Y | X) even for two.

What you will know afterwards

  • why 1/2/3 codings are a trap
  • 0/1 coding and the 0.5 cutoff
  • fitted values outside [0, 1]
  • linear regression ≈ crude probability ordering

1. The three-diagnoses trap

Take the emergency-room example: a patient's condition is one of stroke, drug overdose, epileptic seizure. To reuse least squares, code the response:

$$Y = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if stroke} \\ 2 & \text{if drug overdose} \\ 3 & \text{if epileptic seizure} \end{cases}$$

Now ask what the model is being told. Not merely that the three conditions are different — that drug overdose sits between stroke and seizure, and that stroke-to-overdose is the same size step as overdose-to-seizure. Nothing in the medicine says that. And the coding is arbitrary: recode as

$$Y = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if epileptic seizure} \\ 2 & \text{if stroke} \\ 3 & \text{if drug overdose} \end{cases}$$

and you get "stroke is now the middle condition, overdose the extreme" — a fundamentally different linear model making different predictions on the same patients. Same data, same method, opposite story — purely an artifact of a labelling choice.

When would 1/2/3 be fine?

Only when the categories genuinely have an order with roughly equal gaps — the book's example is severity: mild, moderate, severe. Then the numbers mean what they say. Qualitative responses with more than two unordered levels have no natural numeric form at all, so there is no honest way to hand them to a regression.

2. Two classes almost work — then wander out of range

With exactly two classes the situation is, the book says, better. Code one class 0 and the other 1 (the dummy-variable trick from Section 3.3.1), fit least squares, predict class 1 when Ŷ > 0.5. Two pleasant facts:

  • Flipping the coding is harmless. Swap which class is 0 and which is 1 and the final classifications come out the same.
  • The line sneaks in a probability. For a 0/1 response, the fitted value Xβ̂ is in fact an estimate of Pr(class = 1 | X) — so linear regression is quietly estimating probabilities anyway.

But the second fact contains the flaw: nothing constrains that estimate to [0, 1]. For low balances Figure 4.2's left panel shows the fitted line dipping below zero — a negative probability of default — and at sufficiently large balances it would exceed 1. The book is careful not to overstate the damage: the values still order people correctly and work as "crude probability estimates", and the classifications they produce turn out to be identical to what LDA gives (Section 4.4). There is a subtler failure hiding in the outlier behaviour: because the line must stay straight, it can be rotated by distant points without bound, so the region where it crosses 0.5 — your actual classifier — moves further than any change in the data near the boundary warrants.

3. The book's two reasons, verbatim in spirit

Section 4.2 closes by naming exactly two reasons not to classify with a regression method:

  1. (a) A regression method cannot accommodate a qualitative response with more than two classes — there is no coding that does not invent an order.
  2. (b) A regression method will not provide meaningful estimates of Pr(Y | X) — even with two classes, the fitted values escape [0, 1], and no one wants a −12% chance of default.

So use a method built for qualitative responses. For two classes that is logistic regression, the subject of 4.3; for more classes, the generative methods of 4.4 (LDA, QDA, naive Bayes) and multinomial logistic regression.

Section 4.2 in one line

Coding categories as numbers either invents an order that does not exist (3+ classes) or lets the fitted line walk out of the [0, 1] probability range (2 classes) — and since both problems are about the model's shape, the fix is a differently shaped model, which is what logistic regression is.
